# Day 25: When Emotions Hit

*Anger is not your enemy. Your autopilot is.*

**Tags:** #Buddhism #Chan #Emotions #Anger #Mindfulness

> **Today's Chan Line**
> "If you want to tread the path of the Buddha, don't seek outside. When a thought of anger arises, just see it."
> The seeing is enough. You don't need to fix it. You need to notice it before it notices you.

---

I used to think Buddhism wanted me to stop feeling angry. Like the goal was this placid, unshakeable calm where nothing bothered me. A kind of emotional flatline.

That's not what the texts describe. At all.

Look, the historical Buddha got angry. There are suttas where he's clearly irritated — with monks who misrepresent his teaching, with people who ask disingenuous questions. He didn't pretend not to feel it. He named it, chose his response, and moved on.

The Chan (Zen) tradition is even less interested in emotional suppression. There's a story about Deshan Xuanjian, a Tang dynasty Chan master famous for his ferocious teaching style. He'd hit students with a staff. He'd shout. One account describes him as "a tiger in the Dharma hall."

But here's the thing: Deshan wasn't out of control. He was using that energy deliberately. The anger was fuel, not the driver.

This cracked something open for me. Emotions aren't the problem. The problem is what happens in the gap — or rather, what doesn't happen. Between the trigger and the reaction, most of us have no gap at all. Someone says something. Something happens. We react.

The practice is inserting a sliver of space into that seam.

Three steps. Same structure as yesterday.

**Step one: Stop**

When you feel an emotion rising — anger, fear, shame, whatever — stop.

Physically, if you can. Freeze for two seconds. Don't say anything. Don't send the text. Don't slam the door. Just... wait.

This is the hardest step. The body wants to move. The mouth wants to speak. Stopping feels wrong, almost painful. It is, in a way — you're interrupting a well-worn neural pathway mid-sprint.

If you can't stop physically, stop internally. Pause the internal monologue for a breath.

**Step two: Label**

Name it. "This is anger." Not "I am angry" — that's identification. "This is anger" — that's observation.

There's a difference. "I am angry" means the anger is you. "This is anger" means the anger is a visitor. A visitor you can watch.

Buddhist psychology lists the mental factors with clinical precision. Anger (dosa) is one. Attachment (lobha) is another. Delusion (moha) a third. The simple act of labeling — of recognizing "ah, this is the one called anger" — already creates distance. You're no longer inside it. You're looking at it.

**Step three: Watch the Body**

Anger lives in the body. Always. Heat in the chest. Tightness in the jaw. Pressure behind the eyes. Shoulders creeping up.

Find it. Where is this emotion, physically? Not what you think about it. Where do you feel it?

Describe it to yourself: "Heat, tight, pressure, warm." Just the physical words. No story.

This is not suppression. You're not trying to make the anger go away. You're watching it like you'd watch a thunderstorm — from inside the house. The storm is real. The storm is happening. But you're not standing in the rain.

---

I tried this last week when my kid's school called about something minor that my brain immediately escalated to catastrophic. Phone down. Hand frozen mid-reach. "This is anxiety." Chest: tight. Stomach: cold knot. Breathing: shallow.

The anxiety didn't disappear. But I didn't spiral either. The spiral needs an unobserved mind to feed on. Observation starves it. Not all the way — but enough. Enough that I called the school back five minutes later and handled it fine.

The emotion passed. They always do. The problem was never the anger or the fear. The problem was that I'd become it every time, without even noticing the transformation had happened.

Deshan's rage became his teaching. Not because he suppressed it. Because he knew it so well he could use it.

That's the goal. Not no emotion. Close relationship with emotion.

---

¹ Deshan Xuanjian (德山宣鉴, 780-865 CE) appears in key koan collections including the *Blue Cliff Record* and *Gateless Gate*. Case 13 of the *Gateless Gate* records him saying: "Whether you can speak or not, thirty blows." His fierce style was deliberate pedagogy, not personal rage.

² The distinction between "I am angry" and "this is anger" reflects the Buddhist no-self (anattā) doctrine. Emotions are not self; they are conditioned phenomena that arise and pass away.
