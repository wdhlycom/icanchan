# Day 3: Buddhism — Probably Not What You Think

*When most people hear "Buddhism," what comes to mind has almost nothing to do with the real thing. Here's what it actually is.*

**Tags:** #Buddhism #Chan #Philosophy #History #Mindfulness

---

Say the word "Buddhism."

What shows up? Incense. Shaved heads. Chanting. Old ladies bowing to golden statues. Maybe "Buddha-like youth" if you follow Chinese pop culture — the whole "whatever, nothing matters, just lie down" aesthetic.

None of this is Buddhism. The incense stuff is folk religion. The "whatever" stuff is pop culture wearing a Buddhist hat. The actual thing — what a guy named Siddhartha figured out 2,600 years ago — is something completely different.

## A question nobody was asking

Northern India. Foothills of the Himalayas. A prince named Siddhartha leaves his palace. For the first time in his sheltered life, he sees four things: a sick person. An old person. A corpse. A wandering ascetic.

He's 29. He'd never seen illness, aging, or death up close before. His father had made sure of that.

But now he has. And he can't un-see it.

One question eats at him: **Why do we suffer? Can it actually stop?**

Most religions answer this in about three words. "You sinned." "God's will." Siddhartha didn't buy either of those. He wanted to find out for himself.

So he left. Walked away from the palace, his wife, his newborn son, the entire life that had been arranged for him. Not running from something. Running toward an answer.

## Six years. Zero results.

He tried extreme asceticism. One grain of rice a day. Meditating until his bones showed through his skin. Six years of this. At the end he was basically a skeleton. Still no answer.

And then he heard something. A musician on the riverbank, teaching a student: "If the string is too tight, it breaks. Too loose, no sound."

Something clicked. The extremes don't work. The palace was too loose. The starvation was too tight. There has to be a middle.

He sat under a tree. A fig tree, later called the Bodhi tree. And he did something remarkably simple. He watched his own breath. His own thoughts. His own body sensations. Not escaping anything. Not clinging to anything. Just watching.

Seven days, the stories say. And then something happened that he'd spend the rest of his life trying to explain.

## What he saw

Three things. These became the foundation of everything.

**First: everything changes.** Nothing stays the same. Including "me." The person I was ten years ago and the person I am now — different body, different thoughts, different everything. The idea of a fixed, permanent self is just that. An idea.

**Second: suffering comes from grasping.** We naturally reach for good things and try to hold them. We naturally push away bad things and try to escape. But the world doesn't cooperate. It keeps moving. So we suffer. The reaching and the pushing are the problem, not what we're reaching for or pushing against.

**Third: there is a way out.** Not by numbing yourself. Not by pretending nothing matters. By changing how you relate to what's happening. Stop grasping. Stop pushing. Just observe. He called that state nirvana.

I used to think nirvana meant some kind of cosmic vanishing act. It doesn't. It's closer to this. You're angry. Someone asks "are you angry right now?" For a split second, you pause. The anger is still there. But you're looking at it instead of being inside it. And just that — just that tiny gap between feeling and reacting — changes everything.

That gap. That pause. Buddhism is about training that into something stable.

## Tools, not beliefs

Here's the part I find genuinely radical.

The Buddha said: "Monks, my teachings are like a raft. You use it to cross the river. Once you're across, you leave the raft behind. You don't carry it on your head."

Read that again. He's saying: even my own teachings are temporary tools. Not sacred objects. Not eternal truths. Tools. Use them, then let them go.

Modern psychology took about a hundred years and thousands of experiments to arrive at basically the same place. Emotions can be observed. Observing them changes them. Repeated practice builds new mental habits. That's the engine of CBT. The Buddha was describing it in 500 BCE.

Not as a belief system. As a manual.

> **Today's Chan Line**
>
> "The past mind cannot be grasped. The present mind cannot be grasped. The future mind cannot be grasped." — *Diamond Sutra*
>
> The past is gone. The future isn't here. The present turns into the past the moment you notice it. This sounds bleak. It's not. If nothing can be held onto, what exactly are we so anxious about?
