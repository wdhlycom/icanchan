# Day 8: Who Was the Buddha, Actually?

*Not a god. Not a prophet. A guy who saw something he couldn't un-see, walked away from everything, and figured out how to look at his own mind.*

**Tags:** #Buddhism #Chan #History #Buddha #Philosophy

---

Week two. We're pulling the camera back. Last week was the framework. This week: how the whole thing happened. Where it came from. Who started it.

So. Siddhartha Gautama. Northern India. Himalayas. 2,600 years ago.

## The best life money could buy

By the standards of his time, Siddhartha won the lottery at birth. His father ruled a small kingdom — more like a city-state, maybe the size of a county today. Palace. Wife. Child. Food, music, dancing girls. Everything a person could want, delivered.

His father kept him locked in. Literally. Sick people, old people, dead bodies — banished from sight. The prince was not to know that the world contains suffering.

It sounds like an overprotective parent story. But honestly — isn't this basically the setup a lot of us were handed? A life arranged to look perfect. All the visible boxes checked. And somewhere underneath, something's off.

Siddhartha was 29 when he finally left the palace.

## Four things. One decision.

Four trips outside. Four things he'd never seen.

A sick body. An old man, bent and fragile. A corpse, rotting. And a wandering ascetic — someone who'd left everything to search for truth.

The first three told him: aging, sickness, death. They're coming. Doesn't matter how rich you are. How good a person you've been. None of that changes the basic facts.

The fourth image gave him a different possibility. Maybe someone had already figured out how to deal with this.

So he made a choice that must have looked insane to everyone around him. He left the palace. Shaved his head. Gave up his claim to the throne. Walked into the forest. Not running away. Running toward an answer.

## Six years. Nothing worked.

He studied with the best meditation teachers of his era. Reached incredibly deep states of concentration. But there was a problem. **The concentration would fade.** Calm on the cushion. Same old turbulence the moment he stood up.

So he switched strategies. Extreme asceticism. One grain of rice a day. Sun exposure. Starvation until his ribs showed like a xylophone. Six years. At the end he was practically a skeleton. Still hadn't found it.

Then he heard something. A musician on the river, teaching a student: "If the string is too tight, it breaks. Too loose, no sound."

That line hit him. He realized the ascetic path and the palace path were the same thing — both extremes. He gave up the starvation, walked to a fig tree, sat down, and decided he wasn't getting up until he figured this out.

## He watched himself

What he did under that tree wasn't prayer. Wasn't chanting. He did something incredibly simple and incredibly hard. **He watched his own breath. His body sensations. Every thought that came and went. No escaping. No clinging. Just observing.**

Seven days, the stories say. Then something shifted.

Whatever we make of the word "enlightenment" — the process itself deserves attention. He wasn't chosen by a god. Didn't receive a magical download. He sat there, using the most basic method imaginable, and looked at his own mind until he saw how it worked.

His first words after:

"How extraordinary. All beings already have this capacity for clarity. They just can't realize it — because of their tangled thoughts and attachments."

Translation: **everyone's already got what they need. It's just buried under noise. We don't need to become something new. We need to see through what's covering us.**

That's not a theology. That's a starting position. And it determines everything Buddhism did afterward.

> **Today's Chan Line**
