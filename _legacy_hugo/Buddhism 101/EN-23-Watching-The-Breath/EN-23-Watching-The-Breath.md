# Day 23: Watching the Breath

*A two-minute exercise nobody can fail*

**Tags:** #Buddhism #Chan #Meditation #Breath #Practice

> **Today's Chan Line**
> "The mind is like a wild ox. Tie it to the post of the breath."
> You don't tame the ox by yelling at it. You tie the rope and wait. The breath is the post.

---

Most meditation instructions are too long.

I mean this. I've read instructions that run three pages before you even sit down. Posture. Cushion height. Hand position. Tongue placement. Eyes open or closed. By the time you finish reading, you've already failed — because now meditation is a thing you read about instead of a thing you do.

So here's a breath exercise. It takes two minutes. If you mess it up, it still works. If you forget the instructions, it still works. The only way to fail is not to try.

Three steps.

**Step one: Locate (10 seconds)**

Sit however you're sitting. Don't adjust anything. Close your eyes or don't — I keep mine open, unfocused, resting on a point on the wall.

Now: where do you feel your breath?

Not where should you feel it. Where do you actually feel it. For me it's the tip of my nose. For someone else it might be the chest rising. For another person the belly. Don't move your awareness around searching. Just notice where the sensation is strongest.

That's it for step one. Ten seconds.

**Step two: Follow one breath (30 seconds)**

Pick a breath. Any breath. Watch it come in. Watch it pause. Watch it go out.

That's one.

Don't control it. Don't deepen it. Don't count it. Just follow it from the beginning of the inhale to the end of the exhale.

Your mind will wander. Guaranteed. Within five seconds for most people. You'll think about lunch or an email or whether you're doing this right.

When you notice — and this is the key instruction — don't get annoyed. Don't start over. Just come back to the breath. That moment of return is the whole exercise. The wandering was never a problem. The returning is the work.

**Step three: Count to ten (2 minutes)**

Now count your breaths. One on the inhale. Still one on the exhale. Or if you prefer, one per full cycle — inhale-exhale is one.

Count to ten.

If you lose count — and you will — start at one.

If you get to ten — and you might — start at one again.

That's the full exercise.

---

I did this yesterday while waiting for water to boil. Two minutes. Lost count at three. Started over. Lost count at six. Water boiled. I turned off the stove and finished the ten.

It wasn't peaceful. It wasn't transcendent. My mind kept wanting to plan dinner. But afterward — maybe five minutes later — I noticed my shoulders had dropped. I hadn't told them to. They just weren't up by my ears anymore.

That's the thing about breath practice. The effects are subtle and sideways. You won't feel different while doing it. You might feel worse — more aware of how busy your mind is. That's not failure. That's seeing clearly for the first time.

The analogy I keep returning to: it's like sitting by a river. You're not trying to stop the river. You're not trying to clean the river. You're just watching it. And after a while — not immediately — you realize the river was never dirty. It was just moving. The dirt was your idea about what a river should be.

Chan doesn't ask you to become a different person. It asks you to watch the person you already are. The breath is the cheapest, most portable watching-post available.

Two minutes. Three steps. No equipment.

If you do nothing else from this series, do this once.

---

¹ The "wild ox" simile appears across multiple Buddhist traditions. In Chinese Chan, the "ten ox-herding pictures" (十牛图) trace the entire path from searching for the ox to returning to the marketplace — the breath is the first tether.

² The "dirt was your idea" river observation draws from the Chan understanding that mind is originally pure (本心清净). The Platform Sutra opens with this premise: the nature of mind is fundamentally clean; defilement is adventitious.

You've been meditating wrong. Not because your technique is bad, but because you think a "good" session means a quiet mind. It doesn't. A good session means you noticed your mind wandered and came back. That's it. That's the pushup. Everything else is scenery.
