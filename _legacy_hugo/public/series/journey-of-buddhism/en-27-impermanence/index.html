Day 27: Impermanence and Death#

The thing you’re avoiding is the only exit

Tags: #Buddhism #Chan #Impermanence #Death #Anicca

Today’s Chan Line “All conditioned things are impermanent. Work out your liberation with diligence.” The Buddha’s last words. No poetry. No consolation. Just: this is how things are. Now practice.

I don’t want to write this one.

That’s probably exactly why I should. Death is the topic Buddhism is most known for and the topic most of us — including me — least want to look at directly. We dress it up. We philosophize. We do everything except sit with the fact that we’re going to die, and so is everyone we love, and there’s no loophole.

So. Let’s try to actually look.

Impermanence isn’t a philosophy. It’s a description.

The Pali word is anicca. It means inconstant, impermanent, subject to change. The Buddha didn’t invent impermanence. He just pointed at it. Everything you see is in motion. Your body is replacing cells. Your thoughts are replacing each other. The chair you’re sitting on is degrading at a molecular level. Nothing holds still.

This is obvious. You know this. But there’s a difference between knowing it and living as if it’s true. Most of us live as if things are permanent. We plan as if we have infinite time. We hold grudges as if the other person will always be there to resent. We postpone apologies as if the window won’t close.

Buddhism says: the window closes. For everyone. The only question is whether you’re going to look at that fact or look away.

Why facing death makes life lighter

This is going to sound wrong. I know it sounds wrong. But hear me out.

The source of most psychological suffering — the clinging, the grasping, the fear of loss — is a refusal to accept impermanence. We want things to stay. Relationships to stay. Our health to stay. Our identity to stay. And they don’t. And the gap between “they should stay” and “they didn’t stay” is suffering.

The Buddha’s insight: you can close the gap. Not by making things permanent — impossible. By dropping the “should.”

When you genuinely accept that everything you love will be lost — either by change or by death — something shifts. The present moment becomes sharper. More vivid. Because it’s not a permanent possession you’re holding. It’s a temporary gift you’re borrowing.

This is not nihilism. It’s the opposite. Nihilism says nothing matters because everything ends. Buddhism says everything matters precisely because everything ends.

The Buddha’s last moments

The Mahaparinibbana Sutta records the Buddha’s death in clinical detail. He was eighty. He ate something that gave him food poisoning. He lay down between two trees. Monks gathered around him.

His last words, as recorded: “All conditioned things are of a nature to decay. Work out your liberation with diligence.”

That’s it. No grand summary. No final revelation. No emotional farewell. A restatement of the core teaching and an instruction to keep practicing.

What strikes me about this — and I keep thinking about it — is the practicality. The Buddha is dying, in pain from food poisoning, and his final act is to remind his students of the one thing that matters. Not to comfort them. To equip them.

He didn’t say “don’t be sad.” He said “this is how things are — now do the work.”

That’s harsh. And also, I think, honest in a way that most deathbed scenes aren’t.

A practice for impermanence

I’ve tried this exactly twice. It’s uncomfortable. That’s probably the point.

Sit quietly. Pick something in your life you’re clinging to — a relationship, a possession, an identity, a plan. Something you’d be genuinely devastated to lose.

Now: imagine it’s gone.

Not in a vague, philosophical way. Specifically. What would the morning look like? What would you do with your hands? Who would you call — or not call? What would the first meal without it taste like?

Stay there for thirty seconds. Let the grief be there.

Then come back. The thing is still here. For now. That “for now” — when you really feel it — changes how you hold things. Looser grip. Less panic. More gratitude, and a gratitude that doesn’t feel forced or positive-thinking, just… recognition.

I did this with my kids. Imagined a world without them. It was awful. I don’t recommend it casually. But when I came back, the irritation I’d been carrying about bedtime routines was gone. Just gone. Replaced by the simple fact that they were still here, and I got to put them to bed one more time.

Impermanence isn’t bad news. It’s the only thing that makes any moment precious.

¹ Anicca is one of the three marks of existence (tilakkhaṇa), alongside dukkha (suffering/unsatisfactoriness) and anattā (not-self). These three characteristics apply to all conditioned phenomena without exception.

² The Buddha’s final days and death are recorded in the Mahaparinibbana Sutta (DN 16), the longest sutta in the Pali Canon. The food that caused his illness was sūkaramaddava, the exact meaning of which is disputed — some translate it as “pork,” others as “truffles” or a specific mushroom.

³ The “imagine it’s gone” practice is a variation on maraṇasati (death awareness), a traditional Buddhist meditation listed among the forty subjects of meditation in the Visuddhimagga.
