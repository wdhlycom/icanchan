Day 1: Abundant Everything, Empty Something#

We solved bread. We forgot meaning. And now nobody can figure out why we’re all so tired.

Tags: #Buddhism #Chan #Philosophy #ModernLife #Mindfulness

Life looks fine, I guess. Job’s okay. Money’s not bad. Group chat stays busy enough.

But late at night. You know that moment? When everything’s finally quiet and there’s nothing to distract you. That’s when it shows up. Not a crisis exactly. Not sadness. It’s more like… something’s off. Something missing. And you can’t even put your finger on what.

A lot of people feel this. Way more than you’d think.

We figured out bread#

Here’s what gets me.

The last fifty years — we’ve made more stuff than all previous centuries combined. Smartphones. Same-day delivery. Entertainment that literally never ends. I mean, thirty years ago this would’ve been the craziest science fiction anyone could imagine. Now it’s just Tuesday.

And yet.

Depression and anxiety are at the highest levels ever recorded. Not a little higher. Highest ever.

The line for “stuff we have” goes up. The line for “mental misery” goes up right alongside it. Same slope. It’s kind of hard to look at that and not go… hmm.

I think the answer is actually pretty simple. Our ancestors needed two things to survive. Food. Meaning. Food’s handled — so well that eating too much is now a bigger problem than eating too little in rich countries. But meaning? We just left that column blank and hoped nobody would notice.

People are noticing.

The old suppliers? Fading. Pew Research tracked this: Americans calling themselves Christian went from 78% in 2007 to about 55% last year. That’s close to a quarter of the faithful. Just gone. And people didn’t leave because they suddenly decided spiritual life doesn’t matter. They left because the answers on offer stopped making contact with the questions they were actually carrying around.

A thing nobody ever looks at directly#

So what fills the gap? What’s even available?

Buddhism. I know, I know. The word probably makes you think of chanting monks, incense, golden statues. Or those “zen” lifestyle blogs with their minimalist apartments and ridiculously expensive meditation cushions.

That’s the merch table. Not the actual thing.

The actual thing is strange and useful in a way most people never find out about.

Buddhism is an empirical philosophy of how the mind works. That’s it. Not a religion, really — not in the way we usually mean that word. The Buddha’s opening pitch, roughly translated, was: Don’t take my word for anything. Go see for yourself.

That’s wild, right? An ancient spiritual founder, saying don’t believe me. Most traditions open with: believe. Buddhism opens with: come and see. I keep coming back to this because I think it’s the single most underrated fact about any major tradition.

No faith required#

Buddhism doesn’t ask you to believe anything first. Doesn’t ask you to accept a creator. Doesn’t really talk much about the afterlife at all — or when it does, treats it as a side issue.

What it says is: look, you’re suffering. Let’s figure out why. Then let’s talk about what to do.

It breaks this down into four claims:

  1. Dissatisfaction is real. We all feel it.
  2. Something causes it. You can look at what that is.
  3. It can end. Not in heaven. Right here.
  4. There’s a path. Concrete steps.

Buddhists call these the Four Noble Truths. Forget the name. Just look at the structure: Problem. Cause. Goal. Method. That’s a diagnostic framework. The Buddha was thinking like a doctor, not a prophet.

Psychology caught up (eventually)#

One of the stranger things that happened in the 20th century — Western psychology and Eastern Buddhism ran into each other. And recognized themselves.

The people who created CBT were reading Buddhist texts and going… wait. The way Buddhism describes how the mind generates suffering? It’s exactly what we’re seeing in our clinics. MBSR went further — it took Buddhist breathing techniques, stripped out the religious language, and tested them in randomized controlled trials. And they held up.

You really don’t need to believe in reincarnation for any of this. You don’t need anything supernatural at all. Just accept one thing: your mind can be observed and trained. That’s the whole offer.

No leap of faith. Just pay attention.

What this is#

Starting tomorrow, four weeks. Each post takes maybe eight minutes to read.

  • Week 1: What problem Buddhism was actually trying to solve
  • Week 2: How it traveled from India to China and became Chan (Zen) ¹
  • Week 3: The core ideas — what makes Chinese Chan different from the stuff you’ve probably heard
  • Week 4: How to actually use this. Not just read about it.

You don’t need background. You don’t need beliefs. Just curiosity, I think — about what it means to be a person, and whether things could feel different than they do right now.

I don’t have answers. But I want to show you something I found interesting.

Today’s Chan Line

“Let the mind arise without fixing it anywhere.” — Diamond Sutra ²

When the mind isn’t stuck to anything, it moves freely. Not running away from the world. More like — being in it, fully, without getting held hostage.

¹ Chan (Zen): The Chinese word 禅 (Chan) is the root of Japanese Zen, but these are different traditions with different DNA. Chinese Chan grew up talking to Daoism and Confucianism. That shaped it in ways Japanese Zen didn’t get. This series follows the Chinese lineage.

² Diamond Sutra (《金刚经》): Major Mahayana text, India, ~2nd–4th century CE. The Chinese title means “The Diamond That Cuts Through Illusion.” The Sanskrit behind this quote — apratiṣṭhita-citta — describes a mind that’s fully awake and unattached, not passively detached. Most English translations miss this distinction.
