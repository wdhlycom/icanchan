Day 30: Your Next Steps#

The map is not the road

Tags: #Buddhism #Chan #Zen #Practice #Reading

Today’s Chan Line “Before I had studied Chan for thirty years, I saw mountains as mountains, and rivers as rivers. When I arrived at a more intimate knowledge, I saw that mountains are not mountains and rivers are not rivers. But now that I have got its very substance, I am at rest. For it’s just that I see mountains once again as mountains, and rivers once again as rivers.” — Qingyuan Weixin The endpoint looks like the beginning. But it’s not.

Thirty days.

If you’ve been reading along, you’ve covered a lot of ground. The Four Noble Truths. The Eightfold Path. Emptiness and no-self. Bodhidharma and Huineng. Koans and sudden enlightenment. Breath practice and emotional awareness. Impermanence and psychology.

And I need to say something important now, on this last day, before you close the tab and go back to your life:

The map is not the road.

This series — twenty-nine articles, whatever insights they contained — is description. Words. Concepts. Fingers pointing at moons. The actual practice happens when you’re not reading. When you’re sitting and your mind wanders and you come back. When you’re about to snap at someone and you notice the heat in your chest. When you’re eating toast and actually tasting it.

Everything I’ve written is scaffolding. The building is your life, as it’s actually lived, moment by moment.

Three paths forward

If you want to continue after this series, here are three directions. Pick one. Don’t try to do all three at once. That’s the old pattern — read more, collect more, do nothing. One path. One step.

Path 1: Practice

The simplest path. The hardest path.

  • Daily breathing: two minutes. The exercise from Day 23. Every day. No exceptions. If you miss a day, start again. The restarting is the practice.
  • Daily mindfulness: one activity per day with attention. Eating. Walking. Listening. The exercises from Day 24. Rotate them. Don’t overthink it.
  • Weekly review: five minutes at the end of each week. What did I notice? Where did I get reactive? Where did I catch myself before reacting? Just notes. No grading.

This path requires no books, no teachers, no retreats. It’s boring. It’s repetitive. It works.

Path 2: Study

If you want to go deeper into the tradition:

  1. What the Buddha Taught — Walpola Rahula. Still the clearest introduction to basic Buddhist teachings. Short. Accessible. Written by a monk who was also a scholar. Start here.

  2. The Platform Sutra of the Sixth Patriarch — translated by Philip Yampolsky. The foundational text of Chan. Huineng’s story and teachings. The translation includes excellent historical context. Read it slowly. It’s dense.

  3. The Zen Teaching of Huang Po — translated by John Blofeld. Huangbo was Linji’s teacher. His teaching is direct, uncompromising, and the translation reads beautifully. “Only one Mind exists. This Mind is the Buddha.”

  4. Zen Mind, Beginner’s Mind — Shunryu Suzuki. Soto Zen, not Chan, but the spirit is the same. Short chapters. Each one a meditation. Read one chapter and sit with it for a day.

For the psychology intersection: Full Catastrophe Living by Jon Kabat-Zinn (MBSR’s original manual) and Why Buddhism Is True by Robert Wright (evolutionary psychology meets Buddhist meditation — provocative, not always right, but worth arguing with).

Path 3: Integration

This is the path I find most interesting. Buddhist practice as part of a broader investigation.

  • Read the mystics from other traditions. Meister Eckhart, Rumi, the Dao De Jing. The overlaps are startling and not accidental.
  • Look at contemporary teachers who bridge traditions: Eckhart Tolle (The Power of Now), though he’d never call himself Buddhist, is describing the same territory. Thich Nhat Hanh’s books — especially The Miracle of Mindfulness and Peace Is Every Step — are Chan-inflected and painfully gentle.
  • If you’re analytically minded, read the cognitive science: Altered Traits by Goleman and Davidson. What actually happens in the brain during long-term meditation. Evidence, not anecdote.

A final thing

I started this series because I wanted to understand Chan better myself. I’d read the books. I’d sat the retreats. But I’d never tried to explain it to someone else — not systematically, not from scratch. The writing was the learning.

What I found: Chan (Zen) is simpler than I thought and harder than I thought. The ideas aren’t complicated. Emptiness. Impermanence. No-self. The Buddha said these things plainly. What’s hard is living as if they’re true. The distance between intellectually understanding impermanence and actually holding your life loosely — that gap is the whole practice.

The other thing I found: there’s no finish line. No certificate. No moment when you’re “enlightened” and done. The tradition is full of stories of masters who continued practicing after their awakening. Dogen, the founder of Soto Zen in Japan, titled one of his major essays “Continuous Practice.” Not “final attainment.” Continuous.

That’s either discouraging or liberating, depending on how you take it. I’ve decided to take it as liberating. There’s no test to pass. There’s only showing up.

Thank you for reading.

If this series was useful, I’d be grateful if you shared it. Forward to one person. Post one of the exercises. Leave a comment about what landed and what didn’t.

And if you try nothing else: two minutes of breathing tomorrow. Just two.

The walking is yours. It always was.

¹ Qingyuan Weixin (青原惟信), a Song dynasty Chan master. His “mountains and rivers” saying appears in Compendium of the Five Lamps (五灯会元), a major Chan historical record. It’s one of the most quoted passages in Chan literature — a three-stage map of practice: ordinary perception, deconstruction, and return to the ordinary with understanding.

² Dogen’s “Continuous Practice” is a reference to his concept of gyōji (行持), elaborated in the Shōbōgenzō essay of the same name. For Dogen, practice and enlightenment were not sequential — practice itself is the expression of enlightenment.
