Day 24: Eating, Walking, Talking#

Mindfulness doesn’t need a cushion

Tags: #Buddhism #Chan #Mindfulness #DailyLife #Practice

Today’s Chan Line “In carrying water, in chopping wood — this is the wonderful Way.” The ordinary is not an obstacle to practice. It’s the only practice there is.

Yesterday I gave you a sitting exercise. Today is the opposite: practice while moving.

Here’s the problem with meditation. It cordons off “spiritual time” from “real time.” You sit for ten minutes. Then you stand up and the rest of your day is unchanged. The practice stays on the cushion.

This is not how Chan (Zen) was originally practiced. The monks didn’t just sit. They swept floors. Cooked rice. Washed bowls. The Platform Sutra records Huineng working in the kitchen for eight months before anyone even knew he was there. Practice was inseparable from activity.

The question isn’t “can you meditate for twenty minutes?” It’s “can you be present for four seconds while pouring coffee?”

Three exercises. Try one today. One. That’s it.

Eating: The First Five Bites

Next meal. Before you pick up your phone. Before you open a tab. Take five bites in silence.

Not slow-motion dramatic bites. Normal speed. Just: pay attention while you chew.

Notice the texture. Temperature. The particular flavor of this specific bite — not the idea of the food, but this mouthful. You’ve eaten this meal hundreds of times. Have you ever actually tasted the fifth bite?

No phone. No reading. Five bites.

That’s the whole exercise. You can go back to scrolling on bite six.

I tried this with breakfast. First bite: I was thinking about the email I needed to send. Second bite: I remembered to pay attention. Third bite: I actually tasted the toast. Fourth bite: my mind drifted. Fifth bite: I came back. That’s a perfectly successful session. Four out of five bites had some awareness. That’s 4-0 against my previous average, which was zero.

Walking: Feel Your Feet

Between two points today — from your desk to the bathroom, from the car to the front door, wherever — feel your feet touch the ground.

Not the whole walk. A few steps.

Left foot. Contact. Roll. Push. Right foot. Contact.

You don’t need to walk slowly. Just notice the sensation of your weight transferring through your feet into the floor. We do this thousands of times a day without feeling it once.

Three steps is enough. Three steps where you’re actually in your feet instead of in your head.

Listening: One Second

Next conversation. Before you respond, wait one second.

Not to formulate your response. Not to look thoughtful. Just: hear the end of what they said. Let the sound finish. Then respond.

One second sounds like nothing. Try it. It’s an eternity. In that second you’ll notice an impulse — the urge to speak, to correct, to add, to win. You don’t have to suppress it. Just notice it. Then speak anyway.

This is the hardest of the three. I still fail at it more than I succeed. The impulse to reply is like a sneeze — it arrives before you can stop it. But the noticing is still happening, even when the reply beats it to the finish line. That noticing — that fraction of a second where you see the impulse — is the practice too.

These are not replacements for sitting meditation. They’re extensions. The point is to dissolve the wall between “practice” and “life.”

Chan says there is no wall. There never was. We built it ourselves by deciding that meditation was special and everything else was not. The monks knew better. Sweeping was meditation. Cooking was meditation. Answering the door was meditation.

You don’t need a cushion. You need a few seconds of attention in the middle of a normal day.

One of these. Once. Today.

That’s the assignment.

¹ Huineng’s kitchen period is recorded in the Platform Sutra (六祖坛经), Chapter 1. After receiving transmission from Hongren, he was told to go south and live in obscurity. He spent time working in a monastery kitchen, his identity unknown.

² The “wall between practice and life” is a core Chan critique of formal meditation divorced from daily activity. Mazu Daoyi (马祖道一) taught: “The ordinary mind is the Way” (平常心是道).
