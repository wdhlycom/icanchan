Day 5: Where Suffering Comes From — Three Engines You Never Noticed#

Greed, hatred, delusion. They sound like moral judgments. They’re not. They’re three automatic processes running in the background. All the time.

Tags: #Buddhism #Chan #Psychology #Mindfulness #Suffering

Last time I said suffering has causes. Not vague causes. Specific ones. Buddhism names three: greed, hatred, delusion.

Those words sound like someone wagging a finger. “You’re too greedy. You’re too angry. You’re too ignorant.” That’s not what they mean. They describe processes. Mechanical things your mind does whether you want it to or not.

Engine one: you want the good stuff to stay#

“Greed” here isn’t about money. It’s bigger than that.

You have a really good coffee. Before you’ve finished it, your mind’s already going: “I need this again tomorrow.” You hear a song that hits just right and you loop it thirty times. You meet someone you click with and suddenly you want to see them every day.

That impulse. That “again, please.” That’s greed.

Here’s the problem with it. The second coffee isn’t as good as the first. By the fifth one you can’t taste anything special anymore. But your hand keeps reaching. You’re not enjoying the coffee. You’re chasing the memory of the first sip. Chasing something that already happened. That’s gone.

Engine two: you want the bad stuff gone#

Your alarm goes off. Ugh. Train’s packed. Ugh. Boss messages. Ugh.

Hatred isn’t necessarily rage. Most of the time it’s low-grade irritation. A constant background hum of “not this.” The mind’s default response to anything uncomfortable is: push it away. Delete it. Make it stop.

But most uncomfortable things can’t be deleted. Traffic. Other people’s personalities. Your own mood. You can’t click “remove” on any of these. So you end up in this exhausting position — one foot on the gas (trying to escape), one foot still in the thing. Double the energy drain.

Engine three: when nothing’s happening, the real trouble starts#

This is the sneakiest one.

You’re in line somewhere. Nothing is particularly good or bad. Just… waiting. And then your brain starts. The meeting this afternoon. That thing you said last week, was it weird? Can I actually afford to stay in this city three years from now?

None of these thoughts have anything to do with standing in line. But they’re running anyway. Automatically.

Buddhism calls this delusion. Not “being dumb.” A basic misunderstanding. You’ve mistaken your thoughts for yourself. A thought appears and you go “that’s me thinking.” But you didn’t choose that thought. It just showed up. And now you’re following it.

The three together#

When all three run at once, which is pretty much always:

  • Something good → reach for it → get it → fear of losing it → anxiety
  • Something bad → push against it → can’t push it away → anger
  • Nothing much happening → boredom → mind starts generating content → raw material for more anxiety and anger

This explains the thing from day one. Why you feel unsettled even when nothing’s wrong. You’re on autopilot. The engines are running. Nobody’s at the wheel.

Can they stop#

The Buddha said yes. And not by force. Not by gritting your teeth and “trying harder” not to be greedy or angry. That actually makes it worse — now you’re adding self-criticism to an already running engine.

The method: watch it run. See the process clearly. A kid throwing a tantrum gets louder when you yell. When you just sit there and watch, eventually they run out of steam. The mind works the same way. You watch the engine. You don’t feed it. It winds down on its own.

Today’s Chan Line

“All phenomena are without self.” — Dhammapada

“Self” isn’t one solid thing. It’s a collection of thoughts, feelings, memories — and every single one of them is changing. So why take every thought so seriously?
