Day 12: When Buddhism Met Daoism — The First Contact#

67 CE. A Chinese emperor dreams of a golden man. Two Indian monks arrive on white horses. Buddhism enters China. Nobody understands a word of it.

Tags: #Buddhism #Chan #Daoism #ChineseHistory #Geyi

Year 67 CE. Emperor Ming of the Eastern Han has a dream. A golden figure, glowing, floating through his palace.

The next morning he asks his ministers. One says: there’s a sage in the west. They call him “Buddha.”

Ming sends a delegation. Two years later, two Indian monks arrive in Luoyang on white horses, carrying statues and scrolls. The White Horse Temple — China’s first Buddhist monastery — gets built to house them.

Parts of the story are legend. But the monastery was real. Buddhism had its first foothold in China.

Nobody understood anything#

The early transmission was brutal. Not because Chinese people were hostile to foreign ideas. Because Buddhist concepts literally couldn’t be said in Chinese.

Example: nirvana. Sanskrit nirvāṇa means “blowing out” — a flame extinguishing. In Indian culture, this reads as peace, coolness, release. Beautiful.

In Chinese? “Extinguishing” = dead. Destroyed. Nothing left.

How do you sell that?

Geyi: using Daoist words to say Buddhist things#

Early Chinese Buddhists stumbled onto a method that looked clumsy and turned out to be genius. They called it geyi — “matching meanings.” ¹

The idea: find the Daoist term closest to the Buddhist concept. Use it. Fix the mismatch later.

Sanskrit Daoist stand-in The problem
Nirvāṇa Wu-wei (non-action) Direction’s right. Depth isn’t.
Śūnyatā (emptiness) Wu (nothingness) Close. But emptiness isn’t nothing.
Śīla (discipline) Li (ritual propriety) Both are behavioral codes. Different souls.
Dhyāna (meditation) Zuo-wang (sitting-forgetting) Zhuangzi wants to forget the self. The Buddha wants to see through it.

Traditionalist monks hated this. Called it distortion. But it was the only way Buddhism survived. A foreign idea shoved in raw gets rejected. Translated into local language first, corrected later — that’s cultural adaptation. It works.

Why Daoism was the perfect bridge#

Daoism and Buddhism share some uncanny overlaps. Not identity. But enough to start a conversation.

Both talk about emptiness. Laozi: “Being and non-being give rise to each other.” Buddhism: “Form and emptiness are not two.”

Both distrust language. Zhuangzi: “Get the meaning, forget the words.” Chan: “Not dependent on words and letters.”

Both think forced intervention makes things worse. Daoism: “The Way follows what is natural.” Buddhism: “Follow conditions as they arise.”

These overlaps gave Buddhism its first Chinese readers. Not monks. Intellectuals.

The cost of the bridge#

Geyi let Buddhism in. But it left long shadows. For centuries, Chinese readers mistook “emptiness” (śūnyatā) for Daoist “nothingness” (wu). The Daoist wu means: nothing there. The Buddhist emptiness means: no independent, fixed essence — not nothing, just no permanent thing.

It took about two hundred years to untangle this. The key figure was Kumarajiva — a Central Asian monk who set up a translation workshop in Chang’an and produced versions of the major sutras that were finally accurate.

The geyi era ended. Buddhism didn’t need Daoist clothes anymore. But the meeting had already permanently changed its DNA.

Chinese Chan was seeded in that collision.

Today’s Chan Line

“The Way that can be spoken is not the eternal Way. The name that can be named is not the eternal name.” — Laozi, Dao De Jing

Whatever can be put into words isn’t the final truth. Whatever can be labeled has already left its purest form. Two ancient civilizations, moving on separate tracks, looked up and saw the same far mountain.

¹ Geyi (格义): Literally “categorizing concepts.” An early Chinese Buddhist method of translating Indian ideas by matching them to existing Daoist terms. Functionally brilliant, philosophically messy. The seed of all later Chinese Buddhist innovation.
