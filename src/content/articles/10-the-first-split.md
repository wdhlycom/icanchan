Most people picture Buddhism as one unified thing. Same texts, same rules, same beliefs, forever. Not even close.

**Buddhism split within a century of its founder's death.** And this wasn't some accident. It was practically guaranteed by the way he set things up.

## Who's in charge when the boss is gone

While the Buddha was alive, this problem didn't exist. Someone had a question, they asked him. He answered. Done.

Then he was dying. His students gathered around, panicking. "After you're gone, what do we follow? Who do we listen to?"

His answer was short:

> "Let the teaching be your teacher. Let the discipline be your teacher."

Translation: **the Dharma — what I taught. The Vinaya — the rules I set. Those are your teachers now. Don't make a new boss.**

Classic Buddha. Even his own authority wasn't supposed to last forever. But here's the thing he didn't solve, probably couldn't solve: **who gets to interpret the Dharma?** When two monks read the same teaching and come to opposite conclusions, whose reading counts?

## The first council: a memory contest

Three months after the Buddha died. First rainy season. Five hundred senior monks gathered in a city called Rajagaha.

Ananda — the Buddha's personal attendant, famous for his memory — recited all the teachings from memory. Upali recited all the rules. The assembly checked. If everyone agreed "yes, that's what he said," it went into the official record.

This was the First Buddhist Council. Kind of like the world's earliest version control meeting. One problem: **none of this was written down.** It was all oral. Hundreds of people's memories. For decades.

You can guess how well that held up.

## A hundred years later: it breaks

About a century after the Buddha's death, monks in the east started doing things the western monks considered out of bounds. Eating after noon. Accepting money. Small stuff. Or maybe big stuff — depends who you ask.

The eastern monks: circumstances change. Rules have to adapt. The western monks: the Buddha never did this. Rules are rules.

They held a council in the city of Vaishali. The Second Buddhist Council. They didn't agree on anything. ¹

The eastern monks walked out. Formed their own group. Called themselves the **Mahasanghika** — the "Great Assembly," implying they were the majority. The western group became the **Sthavira** — the "Elders."

First formal Buddhist schism. The crack never healed.

## Why this matters

Because the split revealed a question no one could answer: **should the teachings stay frozen forever, or evolve with the world?**

This question runs through every Buddhist school, every reform movement, every new interpretation for the next 2,500 years.

The Sthavira side (later Theravada) chose "freeze it." The texts are the texts. Don't touch them. This tradition spread to Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia — what we now call Southern Buddhism.

But here's the irony. The Buddha himself said: "My teachings are a raft. Cross the river. Leave the raft." His own metaphor tells you not to freeze anything.

That contradiction — between the founder's attitude and the institution's instinct to preserve — is the engine behind everything that comes next. Especially the biggest explosion in Buddhist history: the Mahayana revolution.
