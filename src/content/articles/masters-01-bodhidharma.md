At the start of the sixth century, a monk from India came ashore at Guangzhou.

His name was Bodhidharma. Later generations made him the First Patriarch of Chan. If Chinese Chan has a starting point, most people put it there.

He arrived while Emperor Wu of Liang held the throne. Wu was a patron in the way that only an emperor can be a patron: he built temples, had sutras copied, had monks ordained in great numbers. Four times he gave himself to Tongtai Temple to work as its slave, and four times his ministers had to ransom him back. He nearly moved the treasury into the Dharma hall.

The first thing he asked Bodhidharma was: I have done all this. How much merit have I earned?

Bodhidharma said: None.

## Two different ledgers

This was not modesty, and it was not a deliberate insult. Wu was asking how much he had accumulated. Bodhidharma answered that the matter is not on that ledger at all. **The two of them were not using the same book.**

Wu tried another direction. What is the highest truth?

Bodhidharma: Vast emptiness. Nothing holy.

Wu pressed once more. Then who is this person standing in front of me?

Bodhidharma: I don't know.

All three answers were honest. All three also landed nowhere. What Wu wanted was to be confirmed, in person, by someone with authority, to be handed an answer that settled who he was. Bodhidharma's three replies quietly took that self away each time. A man whose self has just been removed does not usually look pleased.

So Bodhidharma left. He crossed the Yangtze and went north, and finally sat down in a cave at Shaolin Monastery on Mount Song, facing the wall. Nine years.

## What the wall was for

Later tellings love to make this an austerity story, as if nine years of discomfort bought him some qualification. That reading misses it.

Facing a wall is not staring into space. It is closer to setting down what is in your hands and turning around to look at yourself. People spend the day busy, their backs to themselves, covering the uneasy voice inside with work, conversation, entertainment. Facing a wall is turning to face that voice.

There is a case here. For years I read it as myth. Someone came asking for the teaching and stood all night in the snow outside the cave. By morning the snow was past his knees. Only then did Bodhidharma turn around.

The man said: My mind is not at peace. Please set it at rest for me.

Bodhidharma said: Bring me that mind, and I will set it at rest.

The man searched. After a long while he said: I cannot find that mind.

Bodhidharma said: I have already set it at rest for you.

## The sentence that mattered

For years I read this and thought about the last line. How did Bodhidharma know? He never saw the man's mind.

It took me a long time to notice that Bodhidharma did not care about that. From beginning to end he did one thing: he asked for the mind back. Whether it was returned was not his problem.

**What actually happened was the sentence "I cannot find that mind."**

Before that, everything was still words. After it, there were no words. No one can prove to another person that his mind is at peace. You can only wait for him to say he cannot find it.

Now look again at the three answers and they are one continuous motion. "No merit" takes Emperor Wu off the ledger. "Nothing holy" takes authority off its throne. "I don't know" takes the person himself off as well. Three removals, and the room holds one man and himself.

Wu wanted someone to stamp his papers. What he got was a scene in which nobody stamps anything. The nine years facing the wall say the same thing: if you are still waiting for someone to give you the word, the nine years are wasted.

You do not need to find a wall, and you do not need nine years. Find a moment, stop your hand before it reaches the phone, and turn around to see what is churning in your chest.

That is enough.
