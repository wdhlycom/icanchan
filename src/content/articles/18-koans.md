Yesterday was the manual. No-thought, no-form, no-abiding. Today is the exercise book.

Chan (Zen)¹ invented something called *gong'an* — public cases. More commonly known by the Japanese pronunciation: koans. A monk asks a question. The master responds. Usually it's baffling. Sometimes it's hitting someone.

The standard Western take: koans are riddles. Mystical puzzles. Think about them and you'll get enlightened.

That's wrong. And here's why it matters.

## Zhaozhou's "No!"

A monk asked Zhaozhou: "Does a dog have Buddha-nature?"

Zhaozhou: "Wu!" — No.

That's it. One of the most famous koans. If you start interpreting — "Wu means emptiness, dog represents ordinary beings, Buddha-nature means..." — you've already missed the entire point.

Koans aren't meant to be understood. **They're meant to exhaust your habit of understanding things.**

## What your brain does with words

Normal conversation has an invisible rule. Every word should point to some meaning. You speak. I decode. We're done. This works for ordering coffee. For negotiating contracts. For explaining physics.

But Chan's goal isn't information. It's a shift in experience. And the decoding tool — your brain's most fluent operation — can't get you there.

Imagine you've never tasted sugar. Someone says "sweet." You open a dictionary. Memorize the chemical formula. Read neuroscience papers on taste receptors. Write a dissertation. Then ask again: "So what does sweet taste like?" Your knowledge is perfect. And useless.

That's the gap Chan is pointing at. Words can describe the menu. Can't give you the meal.

## The moment before interpretation

Mazu Daoyi was sitting. A student walked up and asked: "What teaching did Bodhidharma bring from the West?"

Mazu didn't answer. He hit the student in the chest with his whisk.

The student froze. He'd prepared for a lecture. A sutra quotation. A philosophical explanation. Instead — nothing. Just a physical sensation and total surprise.

And then he laughed.

Not from being hit. From the split-second gap after being hit. In that moment, he wasn't analyzing. Wasn't comparing. Wasn't thinking at all. He was just... there. Alert. Awake. No narrative running.

**That gap is what the koan is designed to create.** Not through explanation — through disruption. The mind's interpretation engine stalls. And in the silence of the engine stalling, something else becomes visible.

## Why this matches modern psychology

Arthur Deikman, an American psychologist, ran an experiment in the 1960s. He had people repeatedly focus on a meaningless word. Not analyzing it. Just holding it. After a while, the word's semantics collapsed. It became just a sound.

And when meaning disappeared, perception sharpened. Colors looked more vivid. Sounds became clearer. Time felt different.

Deikman called it "de-automatization." Your brain normally runs on autopilot — every input gets instantly categorized, labeled, filed. A table. A window. That's Bob. When the autopilot shuts off, you see the world raw. Before the labels.

Zhaozhou's "No!" is a Deikman device. You sit with it. Not to figure out what it "means." To run your meaning-making habit until it tires itself out. When it finally stops — there's the world. Unlabeled. Direct.

## The setup and the punchline

I should be clear: koans aren't random nonsense. They're carefully built. The question ("Does a dog have Buddha-nature?") is designed to hook your philosophizing instinct. It's a real Buddhist question with centuries of debate behind it. Your brain leans in, ready to solve.

Then the answer ("No!") knocks the legs out. It's not a position. Not a thesis. Not an answer at all. It's a door-slam on the conversation your brain was already having.

That's the mechanism. Hook, then break. The breaking is the teaching.
