The story is short enough to pass in two sentences, and people remember it for years.

At Faxing Temple in Guangzhou, a banner hung in front of the hall. The wind was moving it. Inside, the master Yinzong was lecturing on a sutra. Two monks stood watching the banner and began to argue. One said the wind was moving. The other said the banner was moving. Neither would give way.

Huineng had just arrived in Guangzhou. He had not been ordained yet. He stood in the crowd, listened for a while, and put in a sentence.

> The wind was blowing and a banner was moving. One monk said the wind was moving. Another said the banner was moving. They argued without stopping. Huineng stepped forward and said: "It is not the wind that moves. It is not the banner. Worthy ones, your mind moves." The whole assembly was startled.

"Worthy ones" is a polite address to fellow monastics, roughly "you two." So the plain version is: not the wind, not the banner, the two of you.

## The sentence people mishear

The difficult phrase is "your mind moves." It is easy to hear it as a verdict on the physical world: the wind did not move, the banner did not move, it was all a projection of mind. Follow that road and you arrive at "nothing out there is real."

Huineng does not say that anywhere. Elsewhere he teaches no-thought and no-form, and he never sounds like a man denying that wind blows or that cloth flaps. The banner really was moving. Both monks saw it.

The sentence is not about the objects. **It is about the two men who need the movement to belong to one of them.**

## The need for an owner

Look at what the argument assumes. Something is moving, therefore one thing must be the cause, and the cause must be named. Wind or banner. Pick one. Then the discussion can end.

That habit is not confined to temples. Something goes wrong at work, and the first question is rarely "what do we do now." It is "who started it" and "whose fault is this." Both sound like questions about cause. Mostly they are a search for somewhere to put the thing down.

Once it is assigned, the mind settles. Whether the assignment is accurate matters less than the relief of having made it.

Huineng does not referee. He does not rule for the wind or the banner. He moves the question off "which thing is moving" and onto the one doing the insisting.

It sounds like a non-answer. That is the point. The demand for an owner is set down, and nothing replaces it. The banner keeps moving in the wind.

## A story that arrived late

There is a textual fact worth knowing. The Dunhuang edition of the Platform Sutra, the oldest surviving version, does not contain this scene. It also lacks the verse that begins "originally there is not one thing." The wind and banner story appears in later editions, including the Zongbao edition of 1291 that most people read today.

That does not make the story worthless. A story that survives twelve centuries is doing something for the people who pass it on, and what it does is probably not historical in the first place. People recognize themselves in it. Two people, certain that the trouble has a location, and a third who declines to give them one.

In the later editions, Yinzong invites Huineng up to the seat, questions him, discovers he is the man who received the robe at Huangmei, and eventually ordains him. Read that way, the scene is a witness statement for a man with no standing yet. **The one nobody has heard of is the one who sees what the argument is made of.**

## After the argument

Arguments end, and afterwards you often cannot say what started them. You remember only that you had to win, that the other person had to admit it first.

The wind and the banner are still there, moving. Nothing was resolved. Something was put down.
