In the eighth month of 713, at Guo'en Temple in Xinzhou, Huineng gathered his students. He was in his mid-seventies and his death was close. What he chose to say in that room became the last chapter of the Platform Sutra.

They expected grief, probably. They had been with him for years. Some had walked hundreds of miles to sit in his hall, and now the hall was about to be empty.

His first instruction was: do not cry.

> After I pass, do not grieve in the manner of the world. ... Dharmic nature has, from the beginning, neither birth nor death, neither coming nor going.

## The test he set himself

Read that again as a claim, not as comfort. He had spent forty years telling people to see their original mind, to see their original nature. Now he was putting his own body on the table as the test case.

If the nature you see is genuinely unborn, then what is dying in that room is a surface. The tears are not wrong because emotion is bad. They are wrong because they treat the surface as the whole thing. **He was asking his students to use his death the way they had used every other event: as something to look through.**

This is not the same as not caring. A man who did not care would not have called them together.

## Four lines to close an account

He left a verse. It is short, strange, and easy to misread.

> Still and steady, I do not cultivate goodness.
> Easy and open, I do not do evil.
> Silent and quiet, I cut off seeing and hearing.
> Vast and clear, the mind has no attachment.

The first line sounds like permission to stop trying. It is not. "Not cultivating goodness" means not turning goodness into a project for improving the self, not performing it so the performer looks better. Goodness that has to be manufactured is already a kind of accounting.

The second line mirrors it: not making evil, but also not making a performance out of avoiding it.

The third line is the one people stumble on. Cutting off seeing and hearing sounds like blindness, like shutting the world out. It means not being dragged around by what comes in. The eye still sees. The ear still hears. Nothing gets hooked.

The fourth line gives the reason: the mind does not rest on anything. Not on a doctrine, not on a practice, not on an experience, not even on the idea of being a good practitioner.

Taken together, the four lines are a man closing his own account. They also leave a map. Do not lean to either side. Do not stop on any one thing.

## What actually empties

There is a fear underneath most grief, and it is usually phrased badly: in the end it all comes to nothing.

Huineng's last lesson twists that sentence. What empties is not the person. What empties is the assumption that any of it was being gripped. The things you were holding were never held. They were moving the whole time, and so were you.

**So the fear is not that everything will be lost. The fear is that nothing was ever owned, which is a different sentence, and a lighter one.**

He was not only speaking to the tears of a room in 713. Anyone who has sat in a hospital corridor, or watched a parent's handwriting get worse, knows the moment when the surface stops holding. If you can see your own mind for even a moment at that point, death does not become small. It becomes less able to frighten you.

The instruction was not "do not feel." It was "know what you are." He had been saying it for forty years. He said it one last time with his own body as the example.

When someone you love is dying, the question that arrives is usually: what am I losing? **And underneath it, quieter: what was I holding, and was it ever mine to hold?**
