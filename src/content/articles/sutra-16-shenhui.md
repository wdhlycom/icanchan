Shenhui was thirteen. He had come from Yuquan Temple, where the great Shenxiu had taught, and he walked into Huineng's room with a question most adults never manage to ask.

> Shenhui asked: When the Master sits in meditation, does he see, or not?
>
> The Master struck him three times with his staff and said: I strike you. Does it hurt, or not?
>
> Shenhui said: It both hurts, and does not hurt.
>
> The Master said: I both see, and do not see.

## The hit

The question was not about eyesight. In the vocabulary of the time, "see" meant see the nature, the thing Huineng had been telling people to look at for forty years. Shenhui was asking: when you sit, do you actually see it?

Huineng did not answer with a definition. He hit the boy and asked about pain. **An abstract question was pulled down into the body, where it could not stay theoretical.**

That is a characteristic move in Chan, and it is not cruelty. A question floating at the level of doctrine can be discussed forever. A question attached to a stinging shoulder has to be answered now.

## Both hurt and not hurt

Shenhui's reply sounds balanced, and he probably thought it was clever. It is the answer of a bright child who has learned that the safe position is the middle.

Huineng took it apart. Answer only that it hurts, and you are no different from an ordinary person who feels the blow and starts to resent it. Answer only that it does not hurt, and you are like a stone or a block of wood, which registers nothing. Neither single answer stands. And "both" does not rescue the reply either, because it is a formula rather than a report.

## The boundary inside the phrase

Then he gave his own answer.

> What I see is the faults of my own mind. What I do not see is the right and wrong of other people. Therefore I both see and do not see.

This is not a compromise. There is a sharp line inside the phrase. Seeing is turned inward, toward your own mistakes. Not-seeing is a refusal to run the outward scan, the one that sorts other people into right and wrong, better and worse.

**In Huineng's vocabulary, "see" does not mean eyesight or visions. It means where attention is aimed.**

Which makes "both see and not see" a description of a working mind rather than a paradox. The capacity is not in question. Everyone can see. The question is what you point it at.

Huineng finished with something harder.

> If you are deluded and do not see, you come and ask me whether I see. My seeing is mine. Can it remove your delusion?

The logic is blunt. Another person's seeing cannot be transferred. You can borrow a teacher's words, a tradition's map, a friend's certainty. None of it becomes your seeing. **The question "does the teacher see?" can never produce the answer you actually want.**

## What happened to Shenhui

He grew up and did something large. In 732, at the Great Cloud Temple in Huatai, he publicly argued that Huineng, not Shenxiu, was the true Sixth Patriarch, and confronted the Northern school directly. Largely through his campaigning, the Southern version became the standard account of Chan history.

Which is worth holding next to the scene in that room. The boy was told, at thirteen, to stop counting other people's right and wrong and look at his own mind. He then spent much of his life arguing about who was right and who was higher.

That is not necessarily a failure. Someone had to make the case. But the assignment he received and the work he chose are not the same assignment.

**It is also worth remembering that the book which preserves this scene was shaped by Shenhui's own movement.** Communities write their founders' stories, and they write them so the founder looks right. Here the founder looks patient and the boy looks clever and slightly humiliated, which is not the flattering portrait a publicist would have chosen.

Whether the thirteen-year-old heard the instruction, nobody recorded. He was young. Most of us hear it young, and spend decades arguing instead.

So the next time you are certain about who is right and who is wrong, the question is not whether your judgment is accurate. It is: **in that moment, are you seeing, or are you using the seeing to avoid the one thing you were told to look at?**
