There is a passage in the Platform Sutra that does not spare anyone's feelings. Huineng is answering a government official.

The official brings up Emperor Wu of Liang. The emperor built temples, ordained monks, and gave away money for his whole life. The good deeds are beyond counting. So how, the official asks, can anyone say he had no merit?

> Building temples, ordaining monks, giving alms, setting up feasts: this is called seeking blessing. Blessing cannot be turned into merit. Merit is in the dharma body, not in cultivating blessing.

![A temple hall with offerings](/illustrations/Zen23.jpg)

## Two words that look like one

English uses "merit" for both, which is why the passage is so often read as a rejection of good works. The Chinese keeps them apart.

*Fude*, blessing, is the return on a good deed. Connections, reputation, health, a better life next time. It works like a transaction. You put something in, and something is owed back. Nothing wrong with it. It is how most religious giving has always worked.

*Gongde*, merit, gets a definition from Huineng that is almost alarmingly precise:

> Seeing one's nature is gong. Equality is de.

Seeing your own nature is the work. Treating people and things without ranking them is the virtue.

That single line moves merit off the question of how many good deeds you did and onto the question of what kind of person you are while doing them. **Merit is not in the ledger. It is in the state of the mind that made the entry.**

## The answer at the palace

Emperor Wu once asked Bodhidharma the same question: I have built temples and ordained monks my whole life, how much merit is there?

Bodhidharma said four words. No merit at all.

This is not a judgment that Wu was a bad man, and it is not an instruction to stop giving. It is a description of the mind that was doing the giving. Wu's deeds came with an invoice attached. I have done this much, so something is owed.

That mind can pile up blessing forever. It cannot touch seeing your nature, because it is always facing outward, watching the account.

There is a subtler layer. Do good while holding "I am someone who does good," and the deed quietly feeds a new structure. **The work gets done, and the person inflates.** Merit recedes further with every temple.

## Two tests inside the eight characters

Unpack "seeing one's nature is gong, equality is de," and it becomes surprisingly practical.

The first test: while you are helping, do you look down at what your mind is doing? Do you actually want to be of use, or are you waiting for the sentence "thank you, you are so kind"? Both can be present at once. The question is which one you notice.

The second test: can you treat people the same? The person who helped you and the person asking you for help. Your boss and the courier. People you get along with and people you cannot stand. Equality here does not mean pretending differences do not exist. It means ranking people less.

Neither test counts deeds. Both watch the mind during the deed.

## The version we run today

It is easy to turn giving into a project. Donate, then screenshot the receipt. Help someone, then post about it. Before helping, quietly calculate whether it is worth the effort.

None of that makes the help fake. Real money reaches real people. But Huineng would ask one more question. Are you doing this so that people can see you are good, or for the sake of the mind itself?

There is a deeper version. People use "I do good things" as evidence that "I am all right," and then use that evidence to hold down things they would rather not look at. Done that way, generosity can be a way of avoiding something.

Huineng is not complicated about it. Do the good deed. Just do not keep the account. Blessing can still be sought. Merit cannot. It depends on whether, at some point, you turned around and saw your own nature.

The last time you helped someone, did a small voice say, "I am a good person"? At that moment, were you giving help, or collecting something invisible?
