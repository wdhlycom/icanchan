"Mind is Buddha" is probably the most misquoted sentence in Chinese Buddhism.

It belongs to Mazu Daoyi. His teacher was Nanyue Huairang, and Huairang was a disciple of Huineng, which makes Mazu a second-generation heir of the Sixth Patriarch. The whole Mazu line of Chan starts with him. Someone asked him what the Buddha is. He said: **mind is Buddha.**

That's it. One line. It sounds simple, and for more than a thousand years people have kept circling it.

Start with the two readings you hear most. Both can be set aside.

The first hears it as "you're already a Buddha, so there's nothing to practice." The speaker saves the effort, the listener gets some comfort, and it works like a pardon handed to both sides. Examined closely, hardly a piece of it holds.

The second hears "you'll never become a Buddha in this life." Same line, opposite direction: read as mockery, as in, you're nowhere close, stop dreaming.

These two sound contrary. They fail the same way. Both picture the Buddha as a target outside you, either out of reach or already in your fist. Either way there's a distance between the Buddha and the person standing here.

## What he actually meant

Mazu wasn't saying that. In his sense, the thing you're looking for and the thing doing the looking are the same.

Someone will hear "there's a buddha-nature tucked inside you, waiting to be dug out." But that still splits it in two: one part hides, one part digs. Mazu's version is nearer. **This mind itself.** Not a possession. People don't own buddha-nature so much as use it, constantly. Whatever is reading these lines is it.

A little like the eye not being able to see itself. Not because it's buried deep, but because it's been there all along, and there's no place to step back to and look.

## A reminder, not a conclusion

So what is the sentence good for? Not getting. Stopping.

Practice sounds like manufacturing a Buddha, bit by bit. It's closer to moving aside the thought in front of your eyes, the one that says "I'm not there yet." The mind was here the whole time. People just kept looking elsewhere, certain the answer was somewhere else: in the next book, with the next teacher, in some state further down the road.

> The Buddha is on Vulture Peak; don't go seeking him far off.
> Vulture Peak is only in your own heart.

In an ordinary week the line works more like a reminder than a verdict.

The thought "once I get this figured out, I'll be fine" comes up a lot. That "fine" mostly never arrives. What you're looking for doesn't seem to be at the place you were heading. It's at the spot where you first stepped off.

## The second half

There is a second half to "mind is Buddha," and most people only remember the first.

Mazu said "mind is Buddha." Later he also said "not mind, not Buddha." A monk asked him why he had said the first thing at all. He answered: "To stop the child crying." The child cries, so you say the line to soothe it. The crying stops, so you don't need to keep clutching the line. The exchange is recorded in the *Jingde Record of the Transmission of the Lamp* and in the *Wudeng Huiyuan*.

The first half points at the mind: this mind, right now, is Buddha. Easy to understand. Also easy to turn into a slogan, a finish line. That is exactly what the second half is for. **Don't go treating "mind is Buddha" as a target either.**

He cut a student off over this once. Dazhu Huihai came for the first time, saying he had come to seek the Dharma. Mazu said: "You ignore your own treasure and go wandering off. There's nothing here. What Dharma are you looking for?" Dazhu asked what the treasure was. Mazu said: "The one asking me right now, that's your treasure. Complete, lacking nothing, free to use. Why look outside?"

Dazhu woke up on that sentence.

So the next time you hear "mind is Buddha," it's worth asking which time was meant.

Most people remember the first line. The second one, not mind, not Buddha, fewer do. But to read the four words whole, you mostly need it.

It isn't a consolation. It's a reminder. The Buddha isn't waiting up ahead, and isn't hidden in some corner of the mind. It's this mind, the one you're using to read these lines. And the trouble with this mind is that it is both the one with the problem and the only one that can see the problem. Most of what follows here can't get around that.
