The Platform Sutra has two people in it. One is Huineng, who speaks. The other is whoever is listening.

The first thing the book does is not explain a doctrine. It introduces the speaker, and by the standards of his time he had no business teaching Buddhism.

Around 660, in Xinzhou in the far south, a young man sold firewood in the market. He could not read. His mother was deaf and blind, and he supported her. He cut wood, carried it, sold it, bought rice.

One day a customer paid him and told him to deliver the load to an inn. He carried it in, took the money, and was almost out the door when he heard a guest reciting a sutra.

> He was poor and sold firewood for a living. One day in the market a customer bought his wood and told him to deliver it to the inn. The customer took it and Huineng received his money. As he went out the door he saw a guest reciting a sutra. Hearing the words, his mind opened.

The guest was reading the Diamond Sutra. Of the whole text, what reached the young man seems to have been one line:

> Let the mind arise without resting anywhere.

In Chinese it is eight characters. In English it takes eleven words and still feels off, because "resting" does two jobs: settling on something, and coming to rest.

![A woodcutter at the door of an inn](/illustrations/ill-027.jpg)

## The mind that parks

Put it crudely: do not leave the mind parked on one particular thing. Parked, it stops producing anything of its own.

Ordinary mind does the opposite. It parks on the phone while you scroll. It parks on one remark someone made in passing, for half a day. It parks on "I am not good enough yet," or on "once I earn enough, once I get through this, once I retire." Once the mind parks, it stops turning, and the thing it parked on starts driving.

"Without resting anywhere" describes a different way to live. The mind is still awake and still loose. It simply does not stick in one place and refuse to leave.

That afternoon outside the inn, it may have felt like someone turned on a light in the room he had been sitting in for years.

## A slur, answered

The next scene is harder, and many people stop at it.

Huineng later walked north to Huangmei to see the Fifth Patriarch, Hongren. The first thing Hongren said to him was:

> You are from Lingnan, and you are a geliao. How could you become a Buddha?

*Geliao* was a Tang dynasty word for the hill peoples of the south, and it was not polite. Hongren was telling him to his face that a southern barbarian had no business asking about Buddhahood.

Huineng's reply has been quoted for two thousand years:

> People may be northern or southern, but Buddha-nature has no north or south. My body is not the same as yours, but where is the difference in Buddha-nature?

This is the floor the rest of the book stands on. **Whether a person can wake up has nothing to do with rank.** Education, family, money, and eloquence belong to the body and the social position. They do not touch what is able to see.

Hongren seems to have understood what he had in front of him. He still sent him to the mill room to pound rice, for eight months, and taught him nothing more.

## Where it comes from

Eight months. What did Huineng do? Worked. Ate. Worked. Ate.

During that time he had someone write a verse on the wall, since he could not write. Two lines from it became the most famous in Chan:

> Originally there is not one thing. Where could dust settle?

An illiterate woodcutter, sent to pound rice for eight months, came out of that room and produced the Platform Sutra.

None of this was respectable then. Chan claimed not to depend on words, and Huineng taught face to face. What we read now was assembled by his students.

That is why the first chapter is called "Origins." Not after a teaching, but after a man's route.

## The doorway

Huineng's position was roughly that of a delivery worker or day laborer today. He was the kind of person a diploma is designed to keep out of the room.

He spent his life being told, and telling himself, "I am not qualified."

And the line he left to Chinese Buddhism cancels the qualification question. Buddha-nature has no north or south. Not reading does not prevent awakening. A high degree does not guarantee it. At the time, that was offensive.

Most people come to the Platform Sutra for "originally there is not one thing." It is a satisfying line.

Notice what the book actually opens with. Not that verse. A woodcutter, at the door of an inn, hearing a sutra being read.

A book that moved the center of Chinese Buddhism chose that person, that place, that minute. It does not start with an argument. **It starts with a picture: an illiterate man, at a doorway, stopping.**

You have probably had a sentence stop you like that. The book's first move is to show you someone standing at the threshold, not yet inside.
