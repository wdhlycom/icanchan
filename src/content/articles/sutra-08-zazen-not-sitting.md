Say the words "seated meditation" and a picture arrives on its own: legs crossed, eyes closed, back straight, body completely still.

Huineng's definition does not fit the picture.

> What is called seated meditation? In this teaching there are no obstacles and no hindrances. Externally, toward all good and bad objects, the mind does not arise: this is called sitting. Internally, seeing one's own nature as unmoving: this is called chan.

Chan is the Chinese pronunciation of dhyana, the Sanskrit word for meditative absorption. The school is named after this word. But the word in Huineng's mouth has been taken apart and put back together differently.

Self-nature is the other term that needs a word of explanation. It sounds more mystical in English than it works in Chinese. It means what you are before you have edited it: the awareness that is present before you start arranging a presentable version of yourself. Huineng's claim is that this is not something you acquire. It is what is left when the arranging stops.

## Two words, moved

He replaces both halves of the term.

"Sitting" now has nothing to do with whether the legs are crossed. It means that when good and bad, pleasant and unpleasant arrive, the mind does not stand up and follow them out of the room.

"Chan" is not entrance into a special state either. It means looking inward and finding the nature that has not moved. Not making it still. Catching it being still.

He adds a second pair of definitions, plainer than the first:

> Externally, to be free of marks is chan. Internally, not to be confused is samadhi.

Outside, things no longer stick. Inside, the mind no longer scatters. That is the whole job description.

The chapter is aimed at a particular fashion. Some teachers of the time taught people to contemplate the mind, observe stillness, and sit for long hours without lying down, as though the body's immobility were the measure of the mind's freedom. Huineng calls that a distortion rather than meditation. Sitting is not a punishment and stillness is not a possession.

Then he adds something that removes most of the work: the nature is originally pure and originally settled. It only looks disturbed when you meet an object and a thought rises in response. **You are not manufacturing a stillness. You are turning around to see what was never disturbed.** On that account there is nothing to add and nothing to achieve. There is only a direction of looking.

## The body in the chair

Put a person on the subway at eight in the morning, back straight, eyes lowered, and the mind is likely three stops into an argument that has not happened yet. Put another person at a desk, slumped, answering email, and the mind may be entirely where the hands are.

By the words Huineng is using, the second person is closer to sitting than the first.

This is not a claim that posture is useless. It is a claim about what the word was pointing at. A body held still is a support. It was never the thing itself.

Nothing in the chapter abolishes cushions, either. The point is that the cushion has no monopoly. A formal sitting period is one place among many where a person can notice whether the mind stays. It is simply a bad place to keep the whole project.

What the chapter does is move seated meditation off the cushion. Where you sit and how you sit stop being the interesting questions. The interesting question becomes whether the mind gets carried off by what it meets, how many times in a day, and how often it comes back.

A man sits upright for half an hour with his spine perfect and his mind rehearsing yesterday's argument. **Did he sit?** By the usual picture, yes. By Huineng's definition, he has been standing the entire time.
