When Huineng begins to teach in earnest, the first word out of his mouth is not Chinese. It is a Sanskrit compound, transliterated: *maha prajna paramita*.

No sutra reading first, no ritual, no setting of the altar. He opens his mouth and starts with prajna. This chapter of the Platform Sutra is the most concentrated argument in the book.

> Good friends, the prajna of awakening is something people already have. It is only because the mind is deluded that they cannot awaken to it themselves.

![A teacher addressing an assembly](/illustrations/Zen22.jpg)

## A word with a missing half

*Prajna* is normally translated as "wisdom." Huineng seems to have known that the translation loses the load-bearing half.

When English speakers say wisdom, they usually mean that someone is smart, well read, good at figures, quick in judgment, deep in analysis. That is intelligence. It accumulates. Some people have more, some less. It can be measured, ranked, and hired.

Huineng gives a different definition, and it is not about any of that:

> At every place, at every moment, thought after thought, not being fooled: this is practicing wisdom.

Read it again. **Prajna does not measure how much you know. It measures how many times you have been taken in by your own thinking.**

The missing half of "wisdom" sits in the phrase "seeing through." And what gets seen through is not the world's complexity. It is the way a thought loops you in, one turn at a time, until you are certain you are thinking clearly.

## Already yours

The sentence gets stranger as it goes. This prajna, Huineng says, is something people already have.

You do not need to go out and get it. You do not need to enroll, read ten books, or find a master to transmit it in a ceremony. It came with you. It has never left.

You are simply deluded. And the delusion is mostly the belief that it is not there.

The comparison he does not make, but which fits, is a person searching a room for their glasses while wearing them. The search is frantic, thorough, and completely unnecessary. What Huineng does is stand nearby and suggest, quietly, that you look up.

## Food names do not feed you

He can also be blunt:

> People recite "prajna" all day and do not know the prajna of their own nature. It is like talking about food and never getting full.

Some people keep "letting go," "emptiness," and "no self" in their mouths. They can recite the phrases, explain them, and write articles about them. They rarely look down at what their own mind is doing.

Huineng calls this talking about food. You can name every dish on the menu in a confident voice, and your stomach is still empty.

The gap between knowing and doing was not discovered by modern self-help. He named it in the eighth century, and he did not soften it.

## More messages, less clarity

You now take in more information in a day than Huineng saw in a lifetime. This has not made anyone clearer. Often it makes people more confused.

More messages means more handles for the mind to be pulled by. A trending story, an anxiety, another piece of content that says, "you need to understand this." The mind is busy all day and finishes the day with nothing seen.

The opposite of prajna is not ignorance. **It is being flooded with thoughts while believing you are learning.**

Huineng's chapter is not an argument for reading more. It is a request to turn around and look at the mind that wants so badly to learn and grow, and to notice what it is doing right now.

You can check this in yourself. The last time you finished a long article or a video about how to live better, did you feel clearer, or did you feel that you now had one more thing to do?
