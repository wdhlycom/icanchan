Fada appears in the chapter of the Platform Sutra that collects Huineng's meetings with individual students. He had been ordained at the age of seven. By his own count, he had recited the Lotus Sutra three thousand times.

A man like that should be soft by now. When he met Huineng, he made the bow, and his forehead did not touch the ground.

Huineng noticed.

> The master said: when the mind is deluded, the Lotus turns you. When the mind is awake, you turn the Lotus. ... To recite with the mouth and practice with the mind is to turn the sutra. To recite with the mouth and not practice with the mind is to be turned by the sutra.

## What the Lotus is

The Lotus Sutra is one of the most revered scriptures in East Asian Buddhism. Its central claim is that there is really only one vehicle, one road, and that every being will eventually arrive. Its famous subject matter is the true aspect of all things, and the Buddha's work of opening, showing, awakening, and entering the insight that makes that arrival possible.

That is a lot to hold in your mouth for three thousand recitations.

Fada had the words perfect. If you had asked him to continue from any line, he could have. If you had asked him what the line meant for the person reciting it, there was nothing there. The sentences lived in his mouth and never landed anywhere else.

A sutra, in this idiom, is not a book you finish. "Turning the sutra" means the text is doing work inside you. "Being turned by the sutra" means something else is happening: the text is doing work on your opinion of yourself.

**The more he recited, the higher he sat.**

That is what the reversed version looks like from the inside. You are holding the book, and the book is holding up your standing. The practice designed to lower you has become a pedestal, and the pedestal is well constructed, because it is made of someone else's sentences.

## The interview

In the traditional account, Huineng asked Fada to read the sutra aloud, then stopped him. The whole sutra, Huineng said, turns on one thing: the great cause for which the Buddhas appear in the world, which is the Buddha's own insight. He asked what "opening, showing, awakening, entering" meant. Fada admitted he only knew the words. Huineng explained, and something in the man gave way. He stayed, and the bow he made afterward went all the way down.

The chapter is called "conditions," because each of these meetings is a story about readiness meeting opportunity. A student arrives with a question he cannot quite ask. The teacher says something that only makes sense at that moment.

Whether the interview happened as written is another matter. The chapters of the Platform Sutra were compiled and edited over a long time, and this one reads like a collection of teaching stories rather than a transcript. The minutes are unreliable. The mechanics are not, which is why the story survives.

## The same mistake, unordained

You probably do not recite sutras. You read. A hundred books on getting better. Courses saved and never opened. Notes written in a clean hand, in a notebook you like. And the life goes on much as before.

The structure is Fada's. The books are not the problem. **The mind that says "I understand a lot" turns everything it reads into new scaffolding to stand on.**

Huineng's sentence holds without adjustment. When the mind is deluded, knowledge leads you around by the nose. When the mind is awake, you use what you know instead of being pushed by it.

So the test is not how much you have read. It is whether all that reading left you lower or higher than you were. And that, unlike the three thousand recitations, is easy enough to check.
