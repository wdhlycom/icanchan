*Prajna* is a transliteration of a Sanskrit word, kept in Chinese as a sound rather than a meaning because no Chinese word quite covered it. It sits next to "wisdom," but it isn't quite the everyday kind of smart.

![Prajna](/illustrations/Zen08.jpg)

## Not the analyzing kind

The everyday kind is analysis, comparison, inference. It works inside words. You put the problem into a sentence, turn the sentence around, compare it to other sentences, and arrive somewhere. It's useful. It built bridges and courts of law. It also builds most of the arguments you have with yourself at night.

Prajna doesn't seem to take that road.

The distinction isn't that one is good and one is bad. You need analysis to file taxes. The point is that analysis has a range, and some of the important things sit outside it.

It goes around language and sees things as they are, directly. Not a conclusion reached after weighing evidence, but a looking that doesn't need the middle steps. This is hard to describe without immediately betraying it, which is probably why the tradition kept the Sanskrit word instead of translating.

## To the other shore

Buddhism gave this capacity a name: *prajna-paramita*. **"Paramita" means "gone to the other shore."**

Put together, it means crossing the river of affliction by way of this direct seeing. The river is the ordinary one: the worry, the resentment, the low hum of dissatisfaction that follows a person around. The other shore isn't a place. It's what the same life looks like when the seeing has changed.

The image is worth keeping literal for a moment. A river is crossed by going through it, not by studying its current. All the analysis in the world stays on the bank.

## The gap

A lot of the time people aren't failing to see. They're blocked by the pile in their heads: should, but, what if. A decision about work turns into eleven rehearsals of conversations that never happen. A plain fact about a relationship gets buried under three years of commentary about it.

The commentary is usually well-intentioned. It's trying to protect you. It just doesn't know when to stop, and it treats every situation as a problem to be solved, including the ones that only need to be seen.

Prajna is a reminder that some things get murkier the more you pick at them. Set them down, look straight at them, and they clear up. Not because you solved them. Because the commentary stopped running long enough for the thing itself to show.

That moment of "I suddenly get it, and I couldn't tell you why" — that's probably prajna putting its head out to look around. It doesn't last. It isn't supposed to. It leaves a small opening, and the rest of the work happens in ordinary time.

None of this makes prajna something you can acquire by wanting it. You can only stop interrupting it. That's why the old manuals spend so much time on what to put down rather than what to pick up.
