Nirvana usually gets treated as a nice place you go after you die, or else as a polite word for death itself. Neither is very close to what the word was doing. The old translations tried "extinction," which doesn't help much either.

![Nirvana](/illustrations/Zen09.jpg)

## Blowing out

In Sanskrit it means "blowing out." Like a lamp. The flame goes out. The lamp didn't go anywhere; the burning stopped.

In the old texts the image is a fire that has run out of fuel, not one that was beaten out by somebody. Nothing is being defeated. The flame simply has nothing left to eat.

Nobody has to defeat it. A lamp goes out because the oil runs out, or because someone pinches the wick on the way to bed.

## Three fires

Buddhism uses that image for the going-out of affliction. Greed, hatred, delusion: three fires that burn a person for a lifetime and won't let them sit still.

Greed keeps you reaching for the next thing. Hatred keeps the last thing alive. Delusion keeps you from noticing either, which is why it's the one that keeps the other two lit.

You can watch the whole set in a single afternoon: the wanting, then the story about who is to blame, then the fog that keeps you from seeing either clearly.

When those fires go out, the tradition calls it nirvana. It doesn't sound like a place you rush to after death. **It sounds more like the quiet of this mind when it isn't being burned.**

And the flame doesn't go somewhere. It simply isn't.

## Close, not far

Read this way, nirvana isn't something you wait around for, and it doesn't require anyone to die first. It's close, closer than the word's reputation suggests.

There's a moment on a crowded bus when an argument you've been carrying all afternoon just drains out of you. Nothing was resolved. The same city goes past the window. But the heat is gone, and the street looks different for a minute. That coolness is already the shadow of the thing.

It doesn't announce itself. You notice it afterward, the way you notice a room has gone quiet.

## Not a place

People imagine nirvana as the end of a long corridor and spend years walking it. But the corridor is being built by the walking. That's the joke the old teachers kept making, and it isn't a kind one.

The tradition refuses to put nirvana on a map for this reason. A place can be reached, and reaching is just another kind of burning. The one who arrives is still carrying the fuel.

Which is why the question "when do I get there" doesn't quite work. The question already assumes you're somewhere else.

So the word points at something that isn't ahead of you at all. It's what's left when the reaching stops. Most people get a glimpse of it by accident, on a bus, in a hospital corridor, in the ten minutes after an argument ends. Then they go back to building the corridor.
