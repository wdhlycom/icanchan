In the chapter of the Platform Sutra that records how students came to Huineng, the visitor after Fada is Nanyue Huairang. Fada was the monk who had recited the Lotus Sutra three thousand times and still had not found the door. Huairang is a different kind of case.

Huairang arrived with a resume. He had already studied under National Master An of Songshan, which meant he knew the forms: the liturgies, the sitting, the vocabulary. A teacher meeting such a student does not usually start from zero.

Huineng asked one question.

> The Master said: What is it, and how does it come?
>
> Huairang said: To say it resembles a thing misses it.
>
> The Master said: Can it still be cultivated and realized?
>
> Huairang said: Cultivation and realization are not absent. But it cannot be defiled.
>
> The Master said: Precisely this non-defilement is what all Buddhas protect and remember.

## Eight years, or one afternoon

The exchange reads as continuous, but the tradition has inserted something between the question and the answer. Huairang, it says, could not reply. He went away and worked at it for eight years. Then he came back and said the line.

The Platform Sutra itself does not put an eight-year gap there. That detail comes from the later lamp records, the genealogies compiled centuries afterward. **So we have two versions: the answer came at once, or the answer took eight years.** Chan remembers the second one, because the eight years are the useful part.

## Why the question stops people

"What is it, and how does it come?" It sounds like a request for a name and an address. It is not.

If you answer "I am Huairang," you have given a label that was assigned to you. If you answer "I am a monk," you have given a role. If you answer "I am mind," you have given a concept, which is a word wearing better clothes. None of them is the thing that walked in, bowed, and answered.

The question is built so that no ready phrase fits. That is why it stops people. It is not testing your vocabulary. **It is turning your attention back toward the one who is answering.**

Which is what makes the answer so strange. "To say it resembles a thing misses it."

Not a thing. Not a thought. Not something the "I" can pick up and examine. The moment you say what it is, you have pinned it into an object, and the sentence has already gone wrong. This is not a claim that it does not exist. It is a claim that language cannot carry it, and that the attempt to carry it is itself the error.

Chan has kept this answer for thirteen hundred years, which is slightly ironic, since it is an answer about the failure of answers.

## What practice is for

Huineng's next question is the practical one, and it is the question a modern reader would ask: if it is already there, do I still have to practice?

"Can it still be cultivated and realized?"

Huairang's reply holds both halves. Cultivation is not absent. And it cannot be defiled. So the answer is not "do nothing." It is that practice does not add anything to what you already are.

**Practice removes what is in the way. It does not install a better version on top.** The difference matters. One kind of effort is clearing; the other is decoration. Clearing can go on for years and leave you with nothing to show. Decoration produces a very convincing practitioner and changes nothing underneath.

Huineng's confirmation is precise: this thing that cannot be defiled is exactly what the Buddhas keep in mind. Not the practice. Not the attainment. The thing that practice never touched.

## The question that cannot be answered

People ask "who am I really" and "what should I become." Huairang's story suggests the first question cannot be settled by a sentence.

The more precisely you try to say who you are, the less you hold. **And when you stop trying to define it, it turns out to have been there the whole time, walking, standing, answering.**

Which raises the uncomfortable possibility: the question that took eight years was never waiting for an answer. It was waiting for you to stop.
