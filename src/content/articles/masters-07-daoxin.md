Before Daoxin, most Chan monks were wanderers of the dhutanga type: walking alone, living on alms, resting here tonight and gone tomorrow.

The pattern had dignity and it had a flaw. A wanderer answers to no one, owns nothing, and depends entirely on the generosity of villages that may or may not be there next season. It works for a handful of tough men. It does not survive as an institution.

The Fourth Patriarch changed that arrangement.

At Twin Peaks in Huangmei, Daoxin gathered several hundred people to practice together. By day they worked the fields. At night they sat in meditation. They ate what they grew.

Picture the morning: a few hundred people on a slope, hoes and baskets, the same hillsides year after year. No one is watching. Nobody's enlightenment is being photographed.

![Farm Chan](/illustrations/Zen14.jpg)

This counts as a major event in Chan history. Earlier practitioners mostly took "leaving the world" to mean "leaving people." Daoxin said you can practice without leaving, and that digging in a field is included in practice.

The later Chan slogan "a day without work, a day without food" has its roots right here.

## One practice samadhi

His core teaching was "one practice samadhi": tying the mind to one place, not letting it scatter.

He put it plainly: "The mind that recites the Buddha is Buddha. Deluded thoughts are ordinary people." No thought arising, the mind undivided - this mind, right now, is enough. No need to wait for a future life. No need to run off somewhere else.

Note what he is not saying. He is not promising a better rebirth, and not selling a technique for producing bliss. He is locating the whole matter inside a single mind that is not currently scattering itself.

That lines up with the direction Huineng later took. The Buddha is not far away. He is in this undivided mind, in the present moment.

## Why settling down mattered

Daoxin's move let Chan stand on its feet and keep going.

Wandering ascetics depend on a small number of people tough enough to endure the road. That cannot last many generations. Settling down, farming, gathering in one place gave Chan a base: people, food, and a set of arrangements that could be passed on.

There is also a subtler gain. Once the same people work and sit together year after year, practice stops being a private achievement. It becomes a shared habit with a bell in the morning and a pot of rice at noon.

That Chan later grew into one of the largest branches of Chinese Buddhism owes a good deal to this change.

## The practice hall is your day

"Practice" today is often imagined as dropping the life in your hands and disappearing into the mountains. Daoxin, fourteen hundred years ago, said something close to the reverse: **you do not have to drop anything; ordinary life is the practice hall.**

Farming is practice. Sitting is practice. Eating and sleeping seem to count too.

Which leaves a question. Suppose someone says, sincerely, that he first needs to get away from his present life before he can practice properly. What would Daoxin say to that?
