Chan history has a famous pair of labels: Southern sudden, Northern gradual. Huineng in the south taught sudden awakening. Shenxiu in the north taught gradual cultivation. For decades the two sides argued as if a verdict were owed to someone.

Huineng's own answer took the argument apart in three sentences.

> The master said to the assembly: the Dharma is originally one school; people have north and south. The Dharma is one kind; seeing has slow and fast. What is sudden and gradual? The Dharma has no sudden or gradual. People are sharp or dull, and that is why we speak of sudden and gradual.

Read it slowly. He does not say the south is right. He does not say the north is wrong. He says the labels are not about the road. They are about the people walking it.

## The road does not have a speed

A sharp person hears one sentence and the floor drops out. From outside, that looks sudden.

A dull person has to grind. The same sentence means little on Monday, less on Tuesday, and then on some ordinary Thursday it opens. From outside, that looks gradual.

Both arrived at the same place, because there is only one place. **The Dharma has no speed. The speed belongs to the person.**

Nor are the two categories fixed for life. Someone dull with a text can be sharp with a shovel. Someone quick with an idea can be slow with a person who needs patience. Sharp and dull describe the meeting between a person and a particular thing, not a rank that follows you around.

This is a quieter move than it appears. Most religious arguments are settled by proving the other side wrong. Huineng does not bother. He changes the subject of the argument from whose teaching is correct to whose capacity is what. Once the question is posed that way, there is nothing left to win.

None of this dismisses gradual practice. Most people need it. The verse associated with Shenxiu - the body as the tree of awakening, the mind as a bright mirror's stand, wiped clean so no dust lands - is good instruction for anyone who has not yet begun, and decent instruction for anyone who has been at it for years. What Huineng refuses is the promotion of that method into a doctrine about the Dharma itself.

## The debate that was partly a campaign

The labels are later and looser than they sound. Shenxiu was not a mediocrity who lost a poetry contest. He was the most respected Chan master of his generation and was summoned to the imperial court in 700. The "Northern school" was centered in what is now Hubei, not in the far north. And the split hardened in 732, when Huineng's disciple Shenhui attacked the Northern lineage in a public assembly, nearly two decades after Huineng had died.

So the famous north-south debate was, in part, a campaign run by a student. Huineng himself is not recorded as much interested in it. **The man whose name became the banner of the sudden school spent very little time arguing that suddenness was superior.**

There is a cooler implication in his sentence, and it survives the history: anyone still fighting over whether sudden or gradual is correct is probably standing some distance from the thing both words point at. If you had arrived, the question of which route you took would lose its charge. Time spent establishing that your school is superior is time not spent arriving.

## The same choice, smaller

The argument reappears now in ordinary clothes. Which course. Which teacher. Whether to go on retreat or work with what you have. Whether to take the shortcut or do it properly. There is always a fastest route, and there is always someone selling it.

Huineng's line still applies, though it asks for something less exciting than a decision. Before comparing routes, notice what kind of walker you are. The fast ones have nothing to brag about, since speed was never their achievement. The slow ones are not shorter, since the mountain is the same height.

Which raises the question his students kept missing: when you insist that your method is better than someone else's, are you looking for the Dharma, or are you looking for proof that you are the sharper one?
