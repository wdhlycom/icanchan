Around 520 CE. A monk from India arrives by sea at Guangzhou. He's supposedly over a hundred years old. His mission, as later tradition tells it: find one person "who won't be deceived."

Not by lies. By concepts. By beliefs. By authority. By anything.

His name was Bodhidharma. Chinese Chan starts with him.

## The emperor and the wall

Bodhidharma's first stop was the court of Emperor Wu of Liang. Wu was a serious Buddhist. Built temples. Copied sutras. Supported thousands of monks.

He asked Bodhidharma: "I've done all this. How much merit have I earned?"

Bodhidharma: "None."

The emperor: "Then what is the core of the teaching?"

Bodhidharma: "Vast emptiness. Nothing sacred."

Conversation over. Bodhidharma left — supposedly crossing the Yangtze River balanced on a single reed — and went north to Shaolin Monastery. There, he did something even stranger. He sat facing a wall. For nine years.

## What the wall was doing

This looks like performance art. It wasn't.

Indian Buddhism had a strong debate tradition. Monks argued publicly. Winner takes the intellectual crown. Bodhidharma saw something: this approach wouldn't work in China. Chinese intellectuals were too good at arguing. It would just become another word game.

So he didn't argue. He sat. Anyone who wanted to learn — first, sit too. **The first teaching of Chan was silence.** Not the silence of having nothing to say. The silence of having burned the words.

## Huineng: the illiterate woodcutter

Chan passed through five generations. Then the sixth one changed everything.

Huineng was from Guangdong. Poor. Chopped firewood for a living. Couldn't read.

One day he was delivering wood and heard someone reciting the *Diamond Sutra*. The line: "Let the mind arise without fixing it anywhere." Something clicked. He went north to study with the Fifth Patriarch, Hongren.

Hongren took one look at this southern nobody and sent him to the kitchen to pound rice. Eight months.

Then Hongren announced a competition. Whoever could express their understanding of the Dharma in a verse would receive the transmission.

The senior disciple, Shenxiu, wrote:

> The body is the tree of awakening. The mind is a bright mirror's stand. Polish it constantly. Let no dust land.

This is flawless. Orthodox Buddhism. Gradual cultivation. Keep cleaning the mirror.

Huineng couldn't write. Asked someone to write his response:

> Awakening has no tree. The mirror has no stand. Originally there's not a single thing. Where could dust land?

Hongren read it. That night, he gave Huineng the robe and bowl — the symbols of transmission. An illiterate woodcutter became the Sixth Patriarch of Chan.

## Why the two verses still matter

Shenxiu says: keep polishing. Huineng says: there's nothing to polish.

Shenxiu's path: gradual. Work at it. Step by step. Huineng's path: sudden. It's already there.

They don't cancel each other. They're two phases of the same process. When you need to start, Shenxiu's right — just begin. When you've been polishing for years and you're exhausted and convinced you're failing — Huineng's right. The mirror was never dirty. You just forgot how to look.

Chan grew for centuries in the tension between these two poles.
