"Life is suffering."

That's the line everyone knows. And honestly, a lot of people hear that, go "well that's depressing," and check out. I don't blame them. If someone walked up to me and said life is fundamentally miserable, I'd probably walk the other way too.

But here's the thing. The word the Buddha used wasn't "suffering." Not really.

## What dukkha actually means

The Pali word is *dukkha*. It gets translated as "suffering" in English because that's the closest single word we've got. But it's not quite right. Suffering is a toothache. A breakup. Losing someone. Big, identifiable pain.

Dukkha is more like dissatisfaction. A low-grade unsettled feeling. Everything's technically fine, but something keeps nagging at you.

You know that moment when you see someone's vacation photos and your stomach does this tiny drop? That's dukkha. Or when you buy something you wanted, and the excitement evaporates the second you've unwrapped it. Or Sunday afternoon, late, when the weekend's almost over and there's this vague heaviness you can't explain.

None of those are "suffering" in the usual sense. But they're all uncomfortable. They all have that same texture — things are not quite right, something is missing, and you can't even say what.

The Buddha said: this is the default. Not a bug. The factory setting.

## A diagnosis, not a sermon

He broke it down into four steps. Buddhism calls them the Four Noble Truths ¹. Forget the name. Look at the structure.

**Step one: the problem is real.**

You try to make happiness last and it keeps slipping away. You try to make pain stop and it keeps showing up. This isn't a personal failure. It's how the mind comes configured out of the box.

**Step two: there's a cause. Something specific.**

Not "you're a bad person." Not original sin. Three very mechanical things. Grasping for what feels good. Pushing away what feels bad. Spacing out when neither is happening. Together they form a kind of engine. An anxiety engine that runs 24/7 in the background.

**Step three: it can stop.**

Suffering is not the permanent operating system. It's a process. Processes can be interrupted. The Buddha claimed he did it. Others said they did too. This isn't a miracle claim. It's a claim about reproducibility — if you follow the method, you get the result.

**Step four: here's how.**

Not just a goal. A path. Eight parts to it — right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration. We'll go through these later, one by one. For now just notice: he gave a method. Concrete. Testable.

## Why the structure matters

Look at those four steps again: symptom → cause → prognosis → prescription.

That's a doctor's logic. Not a priest's.

A good doctor doesn't say "you're sick, just believe in me and you'll get better." They say: "here are your symptoms. Here's what we think is causing them. Here's the likely outcome if we treat it. Here's what the treatment looks like."

The Buddha called himself a physician. Not a prophet. A doctor of the mind.

I keep coming back to this because it changes everything about how you read Buddhist texts. These aren't commandments. They're clinical observations. "Try this and see what happens."

## The name itself is a mistranslation

Ariya-sacca. That's the Pali. Ariya means noble, but not in the sense of "morally superior." More like "the kind of truth a clear-eyed person sees." Sacca just means truth. So it's more like: four things that become obvious when you're actually paying attention ¹.

The standard English translation, "Four Noble Truths," makes it sound like a set of holy doctrines. Truths you're supposed to bow to. That's exactly wrong. These are truth-claims you're supposed to test. Against your own experience. If they don't hold up, you move on.

The raft again. Even the framework is a raft.

Over the next few days I'll go through each of these one at a time. Where does suffering actually come from? What does stopping it feel like? What's on that path?

---

¹ **On "Four Noble Truths":** Pali *ariya-sacca*. *Ariya* = noble, in the sense of "clear-sighted" or "awakened," not morally elite. *Sacca* = truth, reality. A better translation would be "four realities seen by the clear-eyed." The standard English "Four Noble Truths" risks making these sound like doctrines you must accept, when they're actually observations you're invited to verify.
