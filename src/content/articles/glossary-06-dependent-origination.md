Dependent origination is one of the load-bearing pieces of Buddhism. Whatever the teaching circles around, it mostly can't get past these two words.

![Dependent Origination](/illustrations/Zen05.jpg)

## This being, that is

The full phrase means "arising by conditions." Everything is assembled by other things. Behind every result there is a pile of **conditions** pushing it into place.

Two lines from the sutras get used again and again to explain it:

> This being, that is; from the arising of this, that arises.

Have this, and that comes to be. This arises, and that arises along with it. **Nothing seems to pop into existence out of thin air, on its own.**

That sounds like abstract doctrine, and for a long time it was taught as one. But the structure is simpler than the vocabulary. A seed, soil, water, and sun. Remove any one of them and the sprout doesn't argue; it just doesn't come up.

The formula is almost boring. That's part of its strength. It doesn't need a miracle, a first cause, or a hand pulling levers behind the weather. It only needs one thing leaning on the next.

## Counting backwards

Where it earns its keep is in the middle of a bad day.

Someone flares up at a colleague over a minor thing. Someone sinks into worry at eleven at night. Someone feels hollow on a Sunday without being able to say why. None of these look like they came from nowhere, even when they feel that way.

Count backwards. A sentence that cut, months ago and still warm. A task that fell through. A night that didn't sleep right, then another. Usually several of them landed together, and the mood is the sprout.

Once you've counted, you're less likely to glare at the result and call it a failure of character, yours or theirs. You turn around and ask which pieces came together to make this. Sometimes the answer includes something you did. Sometimes it includes something nobody chose.

The count also usually turns up something you'd rather not include. A habit. A tone you've been using for years. The point isn't to arrive at a culprit. It's to see the mechanics clearly enough that the next round can go differently.

## What the counting is for

This isn't a way to explain everything away, and it isn't a way to assign blame more precisely. The point is that the count is where the leverage is.

A result sitting in front of you is already finished. The conditions that made it are mostly past. But the same structure holds going forward: if you want a different sprout, you work on the seed, the soil, the water. That is a more useful place to stand than staring at the plant and being disappointed in it.

The habit works on good days too, which people forget. A good stretch at work usually has conditions: sleep, a clear desk, someone who answered an email. Naming them makes the stretch less magical and more repeatable.

The doctrine is dry. The practice is not. It's just the habit of looking one step back from whatever is in front of you.
