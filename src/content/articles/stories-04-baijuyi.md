Bai Juyi was prefect of Hangzhou when he made the trip up the hill to visit a Chan master who lived in a tree.

## Bird Nest

The master had built a nest in the branches and lived in it. Everyone called him Niaoke. Bird Nest.

Bai Juyi asked him: what is the great meaning of the Buddha's teaching?

Niaoke gave him eight characters:

> Do no evil. Practice all good.

Bai Juyi was disappointed. A three-year-old knows that. Was it worth climbing a hill to hear it from a Chan master?

He had asked for the great meaning, and the word great had set an expectation: something commensurate with its size. An architecture. A system you could spend a winter mastering. What he got was a sentence any child could recite.

Niaoke answered:

> A three-year-old can say it. An eighty-year-old cannot do it.

## The river between knowing and doing

That reply lands on something very ordinary. Go through the teachings and almost nothing in them is hard to understand. Do not do harm. Do good where you can. Who does not know this?

Bai Juyi asked for the meaning and got an instruction. Those are different things. A meaning can be admired. An instruction has to be carried out, and carrying it out is where the trouble starts.

Between knowing and doing there is a river, and plenty of people never get across in a lifetime. The plainer the instruction, the harder it is to carry out. Too plain to take seriously. So plain that it makes you suspicious. A line that ordinary, and you want to call it Buddhism?

Eight characters are not a summary of the teaching. They are the teaching, cut down to the size a single day can hold.

There is also a small vanity in handling a difficult teaching. A subtle doctrine flatters the person who can follow it. Do no evil flatters nobody. It just stands there, in the road, saying the same eight characters.

## Eight characters, with no upgrade

It runs the same way now. Sleep earlier. Put the phone down. Do not lose your temper. Anyone can recite the list. Almost nobody does it.

That is where Niaoke's eight characters land. **What people lack is usually not a deeper truth. It is the sentence: I know. Now do it.**

He did not offer the eight characters as a beginning. He offered them as the whole thing, and that is the part that stings.

There is a version of this story where the seeker goes away satisfied because the answer sounded advanced. Bai Juyi went away disappointed because it did not. He was at least honest about it.

And the search for a better method is itself a very comfortable activity. It feels like work. It produces a schedule, a book, a retreat, a new notebook. It produces no change.

So the question keeps its shape. Not: is there a technique I have not tried? But: the thing I already know, did I do it today?

---

**Someone hunting everywhere for a more advanced method — could that be exactly how they dodge the simplest line, the one a three-year-old knows?**
