Twenty-eight days. Twenty-eight attempts to say something true about a tradition that's spent 2,500 years insisting that truth can't be said.

I want to zoom out. When I started this series, I had a stack of notes and a rough idea of where I wanted to go. Looking back, the arc is actually cleaner than I expected. Four big questions, each getting about a week.

---

**The four questions**

**Question 1: What is Buddhism?** (Days 1–7)

We started by clearing ground. Buddhism isn't a philosophy or a religion in the Western sense — it's a diagnosis and a treatment plan. The Four Noble Truths aren't beliefs to accept. They're claims to test. The Eightfold Path isn't commandments. It's training protocol.

Key points:
- Dukkha is not "suffering" — it's more like a chronic off-ness, the gap between what is and what we want
- The cause is craving (tanhā), but not just wanting things — wanting things to be different than they are
- The cure exists because the cause is within our own minds
- Buddhism is empirical. Try it. See if it works. No faith required upfront.

**Question 2: Where did it come from?** (Days 8–14)

The Buddha was a person who refused to accept easy answers. He tried everything available in 5th century BCE India — asceticism, philosophy, teachers — and found them all incomplete. His enlightenment wasn't revelation from a god. It was discovery through sustained investigation.

Then Buddhism hit China and things got interesting. The Chinese already had Daoism — a tradition that was already suspicious of language, already comfortable with paradox, already oriented toward direct experience over doctrine. When Buddhist texts arrived, Daoist translators filtered them through concepts their readers would recognize. The result was Chan (Zen): Buddhism with the Indian metaphysics toned down and the practical, experiential dimension turned way up.

Key figures:
- Bodhidharma: "Point directly at mind. See your nature. Become Buddha." No reliance on texts.
- Huineng (Sixth Patriarch): Illiterate kitchen worker who understood the teachings better than the head monk. Enlightenment is sudden, not gradual.
- Linji: "If you meet the Buddha, kill the Buddha." Don't cling to concepts, even Buddhist ones.

**Question 3: What does Chan actually say?** (Days 15–21)

This was the deep dive into Chan teachings. Emptiness (śūnyatā) — not that nothing exists, but that nothing exists independently. Everything is conditioned by everything else. No-self (anattā) — not that you don't exist, but that the "you" you think you are is a construction. Buddha-nature — the capacity for awakening is already present, not something you acquire.

The koans, the shouting, the hitting — all of it was designed to bypass the conceptual mind and trigger direct seeing. Not to be mysterious. To be effective.

**Question 4: How do you live it?** (Days 22–28)

This week. The practical application. Breath practice. Mindfulness in daily activities. Working with emotions. Relationships. Facing mortality. The psychology connection.

The message of this final section: practice is not separate from life. The wall between "spiritual time" and "real time" was always an illusion. You don't need a monastery. You need a few seconds of attention, returned to repeatedly, in the middle of an ordinary day.

The arc, if I had to name it: from confusion to clarity, from theory to practice, from reading about meditation to actually doing it.

But also: from "Buddhism is about being calm" to "Buddhism is about seeing clearly." From "enlightenment is some far-off state" to "enlightenment is available in the moment you notice your mind wandering and come back." From "practice is something special" to "practice is the same life, lived with attention."

I'm glad I wrote this. Not because I got everything right — I didn't, and the tradition is too deep for any thirty-day survey to capture. But because the act of organizing what I've learned forced me to check my own understanding. Teaching, even badly, teaches the teacher.
