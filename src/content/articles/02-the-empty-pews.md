Pews are getting emptier.

2007. 78% of Americans called themselves Christian. Last year? 55%. That's nearly a quarter of the faithful has gone in twenty years.

Here's what gets me to think. People didn't suddenly decide there is no God. They decided Sunday morning had nothing to do with the rest of their week. And if you've ever sat through a sermon while your mind was on your mortgage... you kind of get it.

## Three ways out

People seem to leave religion through three different doors.

Science kicked the first one open. Evolution explained how we got here. The Big Bang explained where "here" came from. Geology dates the planet at 4.5 billion years, not 6,000. A book that's two millennia old started looking less like revelation and more like humanity's best guess with the data we had at the time. Tough to build certainty on that.

Morality kicked the second door. The church scandals. The position on homosexuality that keeps getting harder to defend. The naked partisanship. Lots of people didn't lose faith in God. They lost faith in the people who said they were speaking for Him.

The third door is the one I keep thinking about. Irrelevance. Churches spend most of their time on the afterlife. Where you go when you die. Whether you're saved. Meanwhile, people are waking up Monday morning with a boss they hate, a marriage that's wobbling, and this feeling they can't shake — that something's wrong but they can't even name it. What they're asking isn't "what happens after I die." It's "how do I get through Tuesday?"

The old religions gave amazing answers about the afterlife. But the "during-life" stuff? The actual living part? Mostly silence.

## "Spiritual but not religious."

There's a label now. Pollsters literally have a checkbox for it. Spiritual But Not Religious.

About three in ten American adults use this to describe themselves. Not atheists. Not agnostics. People who still feel something pulling at them. Meaning. Transcendence. A different way to be. But organized religion? Nah. They've seen it up close, and it's not it.

They do yoga. Read philosophy. Find quiet in nature. They don't reject words like "compassion" or "awareness." They reject the institutions that trademarked those words.

Here's the thing，that surprises me every time I think about it. This is a massive spiritual market. Like, tens of millions of people. And almost nobody is actually serving them well.

## Why Buddhism

Buddhism sits in a strange position. It doesn't need you to believe in any creator. It doesn't really talk about the afterlife — treats it as kind of beside the point. What it says is: look, you're suffering right now. Let's figure out exactly why. Then let's talk about what to do about it.

That's not one religion swapping in for another. That's a third option. Somewhere between "nothing is true" and "everything in this book is literally true." A path that doesn't start with faith.

Scholars have called it a "practice system that doesn't require belief." I'm not sure that's exactly right, but it's close. Chan (Zen) ¹ can operate in a totally secular context. No gods. No prayers. Just attention.

## Already everywhere

The US Marine Corps. Okay?

In the 2010s, they started piloting mindfulness programs. Soldiers. People whose job is actual violence. Learning to observe their breath. Scan their bodies. Notice what was happening inside while it was happening. No incense, no chanting, zero religious framing.

The results were real. Better focus under stress. Better emotional regulation in combat conditions. They measured it.

And it's not just the Marines. Silicon Valley. NBA locker rooms. Wall Street trading floors. None of these people went to a temple. They didn't need to. They're all approaching the same core insight from different angles — that the mind can be observed and trained, and doing that changes things.

## What I'm getting at

I think we're watching something historic. The old suppliers of meaning are losing their grip, but the demand hasn't gone anywhere. If anything, it's gotten louder. The question isn't whether people need spiritual life. It is what will fill the space.

Over the next few weeks I want to make the case that the most promising candidate has been right there for 2,600 years. And the version best suited to us — skeptical, psychologically literate, modern — is the Chinese one. Chan.

Not faith. Framework. Not belief. Practice. Not the afterlife. This life. Right now.
