Huineng pounded rice at Huangmei for eight months, and the Fifth Patriarch taught him nothing further. What happens next starts with two verses on a wall.

Hongren wanted to pass on the robe and bowl, the signs of succession. He asked every disciple to write a verse showing whether they had seen their nature. The senior disciple Shenxiu wrote:

> The body is the tree of awakening. The mind is a bright mirror's stand. Polish it constantly. Let no dust settle.

Huineng could not read. He had someone write his answer on the same wall:

> Awakening has no tree. The bright mirror has no stand. Originally there is not one thing. Where could dust settle?

![Two verses on a temple wall](/illustrations/Zen21.jpg)

## What each verse says

Shenxiu says: my body is a tree of awakening, my mind is a bright mirror on its stand. It has to be wiped constantly, or dust will settle on it.

Huineng says: awakening was never a tree, the mirror never needed a stand, and since nothing was dirty to begin with, where would dust land?

Neither verse is subtle. Both are built from the same four images. The second refuses every one of them.

Shenxiu speaks from the side of cultivation. The mind collects dust. So keep wiping.

Huineng speaks from the side of seeing. The mind was never dirty. The wiping may itself be the trouble.

This can sound like an argument, and the obvious objection is fair. Anyone with a head full of thoughts and a stomach full of anger can ask what right he had to say "originally there is not one thing."

Before you pick a winner, a little history. Tradition later awarded the robe to Huineng and made his Southern school orthodox. At the time, Shenxiu's Northern school was the more prosperous. Shenxiu lectured at the palace in Chang'an. The records show the two branches running side by side for decades.

The lines are not only about doctrine. They are also about two institutions, two regions, and two ways of teaching, and the winner wrote the history.

## The night in the room

Hongren read the wall, said publicly that neither verse showed the nature, then called Huineng into his room after midnight and went through the Diamond Sutra with him again. When he reached the line "let the mind arise without resting anywhere," Huineng woke up, and five sentences came out of him:

> How unexpected that self-nature is originally pure in itself.
>
> How unexpected that self-nature is originally neither born nor dying.
>
> How unexpected that self-nature is originally complete in itself.
>
> How unexpected that self-nature is originally unmoving.
>
> How unexpected that self-nature can produce all things.

*Self-nature* is a clumsy translation of a plain word. It means your own nature, the mind as it is before you describe it. The five lines are not a boast. Each begins with "how unexpected."

The clean, complete, steady, capable thing you keep looking for outside has been inside the whole time.

Read that way, "originally there is not one thing" is not erasing your problems. Your anger is real, your confusion is real. What the verse loosens is a premise underneath them: that you must first clean yourself out, fix yourself, and become someone else before awakening is allowed.

**It is the word "first" that the verse is aimed at.**

That premise is hard to see because it presents itself as humility. It sounds like taking practice seriously. It produces a person who is always almost ready.

## The trap on the other side

Shenxiu's instruction is not wrong. Someone who has just sat down with a mind like a storm can hold on to "keep wiping." It gives them something to do.

But wipe long enough and a second thing grows. "I am wiping" turns into an identity. Two hours of sitting and you feel you have advanced. A missed day and you feel vaguely guilty. You meet someone who does not practice and a faint superiority rises, not quite admitted.

Huineng saw the mechanism clearly. **A person can escape "I am wealthy" and land in "I have sat for thirty thousand hours."** The thing being admired has changed. The posture is the same.

So he adds "originally there is not one thing" to "polish it constantly." Not to stop anyone from practicing. To keep practice from being clenched into one more possession.

## Two medicines

These two verses describe two ordinary situations.

You are trying to learn something, a language, a craft, a new way of thinking. You work at it for a long time, see no progress, and begin to suspect you are not the type.

Shenxiu would say: keep wiping. The work is not wasted. You simply cannot see the result yet.

Huineng would say: wait. The feeling that you are "not good enough" may itself be a product of all that wiping.

Both are useful, at different times. When you do not dare to begin, you need the first. When you have been at it for years, exhausted, convinced you are failing, you need the second.

Sudden and gradual are not two camps to choose between. They are two prescriptions for the same illness, one for the stage when the mind is still chaotic, one for the stage when the person is close to collapse.
