The last chapter had Huineng saying that samadhi and wisdom are one thing, not two stages. This chapter is about what that one thing looks like on an ordinary Tuesday.

His instruction does not sound like a meditation manual. There is no posture in it, no cushion, no incense. It falls on walking, standing, sitting, and lying down.

> One-practice samadhi means that in all places, walking, standing, sitting, and lying down, you constantly practice a straight mind. The Vimalakirti Sutra says: the straight mind is the place of practice; the straight mind is the pure land. Do not be crooked in mind while claiming straightness with your mouth.

The term he is using, one-practice samadhi, sounds like a technique. One object, one method, everything else excluded. Huineng empties the term out and fills it with a disposition.

## Where the place of practice is

Everyone carries a picture of a place of practice. A quiet room. A temple hall. A cushion, a stick of incense, maybe a view of pines. As if you have to get there before practice counts, and the moment you leave you fall back into ordinary life.

Huineng moves the address. **The place of practice is not on the mountain or in the hall.** It is in those two words, straight mind.

The four postures are worth noticing. Walking, standing, sitting, and lying down are not a list of meditation positions. They are a way of saying: all day, in every position the body takes, there is no time off. The practice does not begin when you arrive somewhere and end when you leave.

What is a straight mind? Not blurting. Not the kind of person who says whatever arrives and calls it honesty. It is closer to refusing to perform: thinking one thing and facing it, not patching over it, not arranging a better version of yourself for whoever is in the room.

It is also not a license to discharge every irritation. A straight mind is not a loud mind. The test is not whether the sentence is out in the open, but whether the mind and the sentence are pointing the same way.

## The rope you tie yourself with

In the same chapter Huineng takes on a fashion of his time: sitting motionless, blanking out the mind, and calling that samadhi.

> When the mind does not abide in dharmas, the Way flows through. When the mind abides in dharmas, that is called binding yourself.

If the mind is not stuck to some particular thing, the Way can move. If it fastens onto the thought "I must be still," it has tied itself with its own rope.

He mentions Shariputra, the Buddha's senior disciple, sitting in meditation in a forest and still being scolded by the layman Vimalakirti. The story comes from a sutra that enjoys embarrassing experts, so treat it as a story. Its target is plain enough: teaching people to sit themselves into immobility points the wrong way.

Crookedness is also expensive. Every bend has to be remembered, because the version you gave one person has to be kept away from the next. A mind doing that all day has very little left over for seeing anything clearly.

## The half hour

Straight mind is harder now than it ought to be.

You tell your manager what he wants to hear. You swallow what you actually think at your parents' table. Asked how you are, you say "I'm fine" and mean nothing by it. By evening the mind has bent so many times that you cannot count the bends. Then you take thirty minutes to sit and settle down.

Huineng would ask one question of those thirty minutes: can that much quiet carry the weight of a whole day of saying one thing and meaning another?

His direction runs the other way. Whether you find a quiet corner matters less than whether the mind bends again in the next sentence you speak. **The practice is not the cushion. It is the second before you decide what to say.** If the mind is straight, wherever you happen to be standing is close enough.
