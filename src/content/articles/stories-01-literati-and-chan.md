Su Dongpo was exiled three times: Huangzhou, Huizhou, and last Danzhou, on Hainan, an island at the end of the map.

In 1080, at forty-five, he was sent to Huangzhou with only a title: deputy militia commissioner, no duties.

## The Crow Terrace

The court was mid-fight over reform. Su Shi had written against the New Policies, and the demotions kept coming. The worst was the Crow Terrace case: the censors went through his poems, found slander in them, pulled him out of the imperial prison, and sent him on the road. After that, almost no friend or relative wrote to him again.

At Danzhou he built his own house under sugar palms, thatched, leaking whenever it rained. He named it anyway: the Gulang Hut.

Three years on Hainan. He learned the local speech, gathered herbs, started a school, and carried Confucian teaching out to the island.

## What he did when he arrived

He also kept writing poems.

> Turn and look at the bleak place I came through.
> Going back: no wind, no rain, no weather at all.

From "Ding Feng Bo," written at Huangzhou. Of course there was weather. His mind had stopped turning with it.

I have a habit with history: I want to know what a man did after he got there.

Su Dongpo governed Hangzhou twice, before and after Huangzhou. He dredged West Lake and built a causeway from the silt; the people still call it the Su Causeway. At Huangzhou he lived in a broken house and cleared a slope east of town, growing vegetables. The slope was called Dongpo. Hence his other name.

He was not sent somewhere to suffer and then write poems to console himself. **He got there, and he lived there.** Planted. Looked into whether the lake mud would work as fertilizer. Talked with the monk next door. Walked the riverbank at night in a straw cape.

## What Chan was for

A Chinese scholar's position was awkward.

Confucianism told him to study, pass the exams, take office, and make something of himself. But official life rose and fell without warning. One season you were at court; the next you were on a road south. A person's whole worth hung on the outcome. Promoted, you were a success. Demoted, you were finished.

Chan gave him somewhere to put his weight. **The work still got done, the post still held, the poems still written — but the string that said it had to be this way had gone a little slack.** Success did not send him into ecstasy. Exile did not take him all the way down.

Later people called it Confucian outside, Chan inside: do the job the way a Confucian does, hold gain and loss a little more loosely.

Wang Wei, Bai Juyi, and Huang Tingjian all came through the same door.

Wang Wei bought an estate at Wangchuan late in life and traded poems with Pei Di until the two of them had a book, the Wangchuan Collection. His little poems are quiet line by line. The last two lines of "My Retreat at Mount Zhongnan":

> Walk to where the water ends.
> Sit down. Watch the clouds come up.

You run out of road, so you sit. In old age Bai Juyi lived in Luoyang and climbed to Xiangshan Temple to match verses with old monks. He wrote poems about the trees and made the mountain half his home.

Huang Tingjian was one of the four scholars of the Su school. He was exiled harder than any of them and died at Yizhou. In those last years he wrote poems, wrote calligraphy, wrote a Vow Text. One road, all the way to the end.

## The excuse

One misunderstanding is worth flagging: Chan as a permit to check out. Everything is empty, so why push.

That is not Chan. That is laziness. Chan has never told anyone to lie down. It asks you to know, while you are working as hard as you can, why you are working, and to know that the work is not the whole of a life.

Engagement is a duty. Not clinging is a little ease. The two do not fight. Drop either one and the thing collapses.

## Back to me

I have a thing like this too. It will not move. I think about it once a day anyway. Standing on the subway, the same item turning over and over.

And somewhere in the turning I notice: what hurts is not the thing itself. It is my demand that it come out a certain way.

For three years at Danzhou, Su Dongpo had power over not one outcome. He wrote poems. He taught school.

On the train, nothing gets done. I just stand there.

It is not as bad as it sounds.
