*Maha prajna paramita* is six Chinese characters standing in for a Sanskrit phrase. Huineng takes them one at a time, and somewhere in the middle of the explanation it becomes clear that no translation quite closes.

> What is maha? Maha means great. The capacity of the mind is vast, like empty space... What is paramita? That is the language of the western regions. In Tang speech it means gone to the other shore.

![A wide river at dusk](/illustrations/ill-058.jpg)

## Great does not mean important

The great Huineng means has nothing to do with being impressive.

He is talking about the size of the mind's capacity, and he reaches for space as the comparison:

> The capacity of the mind is vast, like empty space.

Space has no edge. Clouds pass through it, rain, stars, airplanes. It has never once said, "I am full."

Your mind, he says, was originally that roomy. Praise fits in it. Humiliation fits in it. Gain and loss fit equally.

But ordinary mind does not behave that way. A small injustice spills over the rim. One unkind sentence blocks the whole thing for half a day. It is rarely because the event was enormous. It is because the mind has been narrowed to the small self that was offended, the one that was cheated, the one being discussed.

**The part that got bruised is not the whole of the mind.** There is a large unused area next to it, never worn out, still available.

## The shore is not downstream

*Paramita* is translated as "gone to the other shore." The image that comes with it is a river crossing: leave this bank, row hard, arrive somewhere better.

Huineng's other shore is not a location. Affliction is this shore. Awakening is the other shore. Between them there is no river that anyone rows across.

It is the same water. Deluded, you call it this shore. Awake, the same water is the other shore. **The ground under your feet may already be the far bank. You simply have not opened your eyes.**

So "arriving" is not a journey. It is waking up. Nobody has to move anywhere.

## The practice he actually gives

If that is true, what do you do? Huineng offers something you can use today, and it is almost embarrassingly simple. He calls it not abiding, thought after thought.

> Seeing all things, the mind is not stained... It functions everywhere, and yet does not settle anywhere.

Whatever appears, you let it appear. A thought comes, you let it come. It goes, you let it go. You do not have to cut thoughts off, which you cannot do anyway. You just do not run after them, and you do not wrestle them.

This is the same instruction as "let the mind arise without resting anywhere," in different words. The mind flows past every kind of thing. It does not stop and take up residence in any of them.

## Waiting for the far bank

People are very good at putting their hopes somewhere else. After this project, things will be fine. After the move to that city, things will settle. Once this relationship is decided, then I can relax.

The good life is always on the opposite bank, and you have to get across first.

Huineng would point out that the shore you are waiting for is not in the future. It is in the thoughts you do not linger in, one after another, today.

This is not an argument against plans or work. It is a warning about the sentence "once I get there, everything will be fine." Hold that sentence tightly and the arrival never comes. The "there" keeps moving, because the mind carrying it has not moved at all.

Look at your own version of the sentence. What does it begin with? Take the "once" out and see what is left.
