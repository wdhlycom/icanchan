People use "compassion" more or less interchangeably with "soft-hearted" and "easygoing." It's one of those words that ordinary use has sanded down until it fits almost any situation, which is a sign that it has stopped meaning very much. The Buddhist version goes deeper, and it's harder to do.

![Compassion](/illustrations/ill-053.jpg)

## Two halves

Take the word apart. One half means giving joy: wanting other people to have some happiness. The other half means pulling out suffering: wanting other people to be free of pain. Put them together and you get the wish that beings leave suffering and find joy.

Both halves are doing something. A lot of what passes for kindness only has the first half, the part where you want people to feel better, and skips the part where you're willing to look at what's actually hurting them.

The tradition is fond of pairing them because either one alone goes crooked. Joy without the willingness to look at pain becomes a kind of management. Pain without the wish for joy becomes grimness.

## Not the same as soft

The difference shows up here. Soft-heartedness is often "I can't bear it." Sometimes it goes soft at exactly one point, the point where the other person starts to cry. Sometimes it's really a fear of conflict, so you give ground and call it kindness afterward.

**Compassion is more active, and clearer.** It can see that the other person is stuck in their own afflictions, just as you are stuck in yours. Because it sees that, it can say the hard thing that needs saying. A friend is drinking too much, and you tell them, and the telling costs you the easy evening.

But when it comes to actually hurting them, your hand won't quite go through with it. That's the test, and it's not a soft one. Anyone can be gentle when nothing is at stake.

The harder version doesn't require you to like the person. Liking is a preference. Compassion is closer to a decision about how to treat someone you don't particularly enjoy, which is the only kind that gets tested.

It has a limit too. Compassion isn't a doormat. You can refuse the request, end the relationship, call the police, and still wish the person well. The wish doesn't oblige you to keep standing there.

## Where it starts

The starting point isn't far from people, and it isn't a feeling you have to manufacture.

That person you find most irritating — the one whose voice you can hear through a wall — is probably being worked over by their own pile of troubles. This doesn't mean smiling at everyone. It doesn't mean pretending the irritation isn't there. It's more like a sentence slowly surfacing: **"He's stuck too."**

Once it surfaces, that's about enough. The irritation doesn't vanish. It just stops being the whole picture, and something in your chest unclenches by a few millimeters.

The practice is small enough to try on the train. Pick the person you'd least like to sit next to. Assume, for ten seconds, that they've had a worse week than you. You don't have to speak to them. The assumption does something to the shoulders.

You can start there. Most people do.
