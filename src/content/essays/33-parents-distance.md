You can't say when the conversation thinned out. Phone calls with your parents turned into the same four sentences: have you eaten, are you busy, take care of yourself. You hang up and feel a small hollow, wanting to say more, unable to find the next thing. For a moment you sit with the phone still in your hand, as though the call ended a little early and something had been left unsaid.

Then you go home for a weekend and notice your father's hair has gone white at the sides. Your mother asks the same question twice in ten minutes, and you catch yourself answering the second time with a patience that costs you something. The word loss rises in you and you push it back down, not quite willing to follow it any further.

![The Distance From Your Parents](/illustrations/ill-089.jpg)
With parents, it is easy to keep postponing. There is time, you think. After this busy stretch. Once things at work settle. Then I will spend real time with them.

Waiting for a calmer month is reasonable, and there is always a calmer month being promised a little further out. The visits get spaced wider each year, and each one runs a day shorter than the last.

But days don't wait for you to be ready. They don't slow down because you haven't cleared your calendar yet.

* * *

Chan doesn't step around this. The Buddha spoke of the suffering of parting from what you love, and it comes to roughly this: the more you care, the more the separation frightens you, and the separation mostly cannot be avoided, whether you are afraid of it or not.

What Chan can offer isn't a way to keep the parting from arriving. It looks more like having fewer if onlys left in your chest when it does.

* * *

Spending real time sounds solemn. People imagine it needs a whole free afternoon, a proper sit-down, a conversation of some weight. But it lives in much smaller places.

For one person it was calling home first that week, instead of waiting for them to call. While on the phone, not folding laundry or answering messages at the same time, letting them finish a thought without steering them toward the point. On the visit, leaving the phone in the other room and sitting in the living room while the same old story was told for the eighth time, and hearing it out.

Being entirely there, listening, seems to do more than any elaborate arrangement.

* * *

The reluctance to think about losing them is there because you care. And that same care is probably better spent now, in these few sentences while they are still in front of you, than saved up for a later that may not hold what you expect it to.

---

**A question to sit with:** When was the last time you truly listened to your parents, rather than going through the motions?
