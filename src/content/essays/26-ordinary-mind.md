Zhaozhou asked Nanquan: what is the Way?

Nanquan said: ordinary mind is the Way.

Zhaozhou asked: can one still aim for it?

Nanquan said: the moment you aim, you have turned away.

![Ordinary Mind Is the Way](/illustrations/ill-069.jpg)
The first line is printed on teacups. Somebody put it on a bookmark. People say it without looking up from the table.

The second line gets skipped, probably because once it is laid out, nobody can drink the tea in peace.

The moment you aim, you have turned away. The mind starts moving toward the thing, and the movement itself is the departure.

* * *

Ordinary mind doesn't behave like a target you can fix in place and then reach. The moment you tell yourself to work hard at being ordinary, the sentence already carries a verdict: this mind, the one you have right now, is not ordinary enough. The verdict is quiet. Quiet enough that you don't hear it arrive. But the mind has been tapped.

And the harder you try, the more often the tap comes.

It is like lying in bed, trying hard to relax. The shoulders harden. Trying to relax is still trying, and the body reads it as work like any other work. Relaxing generally isn't something you do; it is what is left over when the doing lets up. Ordinary mind seems close to that. Not something a person builds. What is already there once you put down the mind that has been trying to build something.

* * *

Which brings back the line people skip. It punctures a common mistake. A lot of people take practice to be a climb toward somewhere higher, so they chase, compare, and measure themselves against the person on the next cushion. But the Way isn't far. It sits in the unremarkable part of this mind. Aim, set a target, form the thought of reaching, and your feet have already shifted a little off the line.

* * *

So the work isn't manufacturing an ordinary mind. It is more like not stirring this one. People come and go, things rise and fall, and the mind doesn't rise and fall along with them. Nor does it go hunting for a place where nothing rises or falls, some quiet shelf to store itself on. The stirring stops mostly because nobody is standing over the pot with a spoon. Just that. Tea when it is time for tea. Walking when it is time to walk.

* * *

Later, when *I must try to be ordinary* shows up in the middle of an afternoon, the useful move isn't to push harder. It is to see the thought first. Seen, it has already nudged the mind — that is what thoughts do — and there is no need to add a second one about the first.

Seeing it doesn't cool the tea.

---

**A question to sit with:** How many times in one life do we try to reach something that never once left?
