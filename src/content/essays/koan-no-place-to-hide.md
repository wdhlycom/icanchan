> A monk asked Dongshan Liangjie, “When cold and heat arrive, how do I avoid them?”
>
> Dongshan said, “Why not go where there is no cold or heat?”
>
> “Where is that place?”
>
> Dongshan said, “When it is cold, let the cold kill you. When it is hot, let the heat kill you.”

* * *

The monk wants a place to get away from cold and heat. Dongshan first goes along and offers him one. Then, when the monk asks where it is, Dongshan folds the offer back up and does not point anywhere else.

“Where there is no cold or heat” sounds like a separate location. But Dongshan's answer — cold all the way through, heat all the way through — seems to be inside the cold and the heat. No extra ground has been cleared anywhere.

It sounds tangled at first. Sit with it a moment, and it seems to be about the extra comment the mind likes to add. Cold is cold, but the mind also mutters, *it should not be this cold*. Heat is heat, but the mind starts hoping, *when will it cool down?*

The cold and heat themselves are not always that hard to bear. What is hard is the muttering that follows, and once it starts, the person cannot stay put.

* * *

Everyone has been in this kind of weather. A difficult task, while you are actually inside it and working, can be bearable. Thinking about it beforehand, over and over, and chewing it over afterward, wears a person down more. The sentence “when will this be over” going around in the head is often more exhausting than the thing itself.

Dongshan does not tell anyone to grit his teeth, and he does not explain how to be philosophical about it. He says: when cold, cold all the way through; when hot, heat all the way through. Let the cold be cold, and do not stand beside it adding a wish for it to end sooner.

Without that added sentence, a person stays inside the thing and does what the hands are supposed to do. With it, the attention moves off to one side and starts wrestling with the situation. The situation has not grown by one bit, and the person is tired first.
