> A monk asked Zhaozhou, “Through the twelve hours of the day, how should I use my mind?”
>
> Zhaozhou said, “You are used by the twelve hours. I use the twelve hours.”

* * *

The monk probably has a chart in his head: sit at this hour, read at that hour, do the work at another. Everything slotted in so that no stretch is left empty. It is a serious question and a common one. Anyone trying to arrange his days well thinks this way.

Zhaozhou does not fill in the chart and does not offer a more detailed one. He quietly turns the question of who is using whom.

* * *

Take one morning. The same few hours can be something you are pushed through: time to get up, time to leave, time to answer the messages, and suddenly there is no time left. Or the same hours can be something you are using: now I eat, now I walk, now I finish this one thing.

The hours are the same. None have been added or removed. The place the person is standing seems to have shifted.

Being used does not require being frantically busy. Idle people do it too. A whole day spent waiting for a point: waiting to get off work, waiting for a reply, waiting until the weekend to deal with it. The waiting also hands over the present moment, paying it in advance to a moment that has not arrived.

“I use the twelve hours” sounds as if he is managing his time more tightly, but on a second look it does not. However tightly you manage it, you are still circling the hours. The only difference is that the voice driving you has gotten harsher. It is the same arrangement with a new face.

Using the hours is more ordinary than that. When this moment is in your hands, you do the thing this moment contains. You do not spend it while thinking about what it can be exchanged for.

---

**If this moment is not a corridor into the next one, is it enough on its own?**
