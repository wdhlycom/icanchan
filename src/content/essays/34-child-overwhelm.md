The shoes are still not on. You have asked three times. The third time your voice comes out at a volume you did not plan, and the room goes quiet.

It could be any of it: crying that will not stop, a sullen argument, a grade that slid, a phone that will not be put down. You try reasoning and it goes nowhere. You try holding your temper and the holding runs out, and then you are standing in the hallway shouting at a small person about shoes.

![Losing It Over the Child](/illustrations/ill-126.jpg)
It looks like the child did this to you. Looking back later, it usually isn't. It is the last thing on a pile that was already tall. The email from your boss at seven in the morning. The bill you opened and closed again. The thing you have been carrying around for weeks without naming it to anyone.

There is an old Chan saying: your own portion of the work is not to be sought outside yourself.

Applied to raising a child, it comes to something like this. What the child is doing is not only about the child. It often shows you the places in yourself that are not settled.

* * *

The pressure about grades may be sitting on top of your own disappointment about how your own life turned out. The fury at the phone may be fear that things are slipping out of your hands. The flare of anger when a child talks back tends to land on an old bruise, something about being respected, or about being the one who is obeyed.

None of this is a way of deciding who is right and who is wrong, or of saying the adult is at fault and the child is the injured party. It is closer to this: when you are full and tangled and tight, you don't see the child clearly. You see someone coming at you again.

Settle yourself a little, and there is room to take in what the child is actually carrying. Not always. Not on the worst evenings. But more often than when the room inside you is already full.

* * *

In practice, some people leave the room for half a minute when they feel the edge coming. Get a glass of water in the kitchen. Stand on the balcony. Let the wave that has already risen go past.

The half minute isn't escape. It is a crack left open in a door. When the wave has passed and you come back, what you say tends to sound like a person discussing a problem, rather than a person tipping out whatever was boiling over.

* * *

Children grow. The trouble changes shape every year or two, and the shoes give way to something else. An adult who can steady themselves first is, over the long run, something a child can lean on.

---

**A question to sit with:** In that burst of anger at your child, how much of it was about the child, and how much was about you?
