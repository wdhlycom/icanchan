"The Dharma is in the world; awakening is not apart from the world. To seek bodhi apart from the world is like looking for a horn on a rabbit."

That is Hui Neng, in the Platform Sutra.

Rabbits don't grow horns. The line is not a gentle suggestion that you might also practice a little during your ordinary life. It replaces the foundation underneath. Apart from your days, there is no separate item called practice.

![The Dharma Is in the World](/illustrations/ill-085.jpg)
Before Hui Neng, practice came with conditions. You left home. You left the household, the work, the ties that wouldn't let go, and you went into the forest and sat under a tree. In India that framework made sense: rebirth was taken as fact, this life was a temporary inn, and leaving was a reasonable calculation.

Then the teaching traveled to China, into a civilization that cared about this world. Confucius didn't talk about an afterlife. Zhuangzi said the Way is in shit and piss. A Chinese scholar reading that only a monk could end birth and death would pause and ask: on what grounds?

Hui Neng is roughly what grew out of that pause.

* * *

There's a difference between *you can also practice in daily life* and *apart from daily life there is no practice*. The first still treats them as two things that happen to overlap. The second says there was only ever one thing.

You eat while thinking about the afternoon meeting. You lie down and replay the argument from the kitchen. You walk to the station while scrolling. None of that proves you have no time to practice. That is precisely the ground practice has to work on. Hide in a meditation hall with your attention somewhere else, and the hall isn't a place of practice. Stand at the kitchen sink with your attention on the dishes, and for that stretch the kitchen is.

* * *

People today hear the line wrong. They hear: no need for formal practice, anything you do counts. The threshold was removed — and so were the walls.

What Hui Neng seems to be saying runs the other way. If you don't set aside a special place, then there's no part of the day left outside. Work, school runs, the mortgage, the colleague who always finds something to criticize — none of it has a halftime, and none of it gives leave.

* * *

Someone washing up doesn't rush to file it as time taken away from practice. Just washing. Water over the hands, the rim of the bowl cool. The attention drifts to tomorrow. You notice it drifted. You bring it back.

That repeated coming back is probably the awakening in *not apart from the world*.

---

**A question to sit with:** If there is no separate thing called practice, then the thing in front of you today — which one is it?
