You've been thinking about quitting for four months. In the car on the way in it is obvious: you have to go. By eleven, with the third meeting starting and someone asking a question you can actually answer, leaving seems reckless. At night you open the job board, scroll past eleven roles that are clearly worse, and close the laptop.

You've asked three friends. You've read the threads. You've built the spreadsheet, with columns for salary, commute, growth, manager quality, and a column of weighted scores you stopped trusting halfway through. The spreadsheet is now more confusing than the decision.

You're stuck on the word choose, going around it.

![Should You Quit](/illustrations/ill-095.jpg)
Most people assume the problem is information. One more data point, one more conversation with someone who left, one more look at the market, and the answer will rise to the surface on its own.

It doesn't work that way. More material usually produces more confusion, because the material isn't competing on facts — it's competing on fears. What keeps you from deciding isn't that you don't know enough. It's that you're afraid. Afraid of picking wrong. Afraid of the version of yourself eighteen months out who regrets it. Afraid of the specific Tuesday when the what-if actually lands and you have to live in it.

* * *

Chan has a line about choosing that people find either relieving or infuriating: no path is exactly right. There is no option sitting out there that is correct down to the decimal.

That sounds unhelpful, and it is also accurate. Every path has a smooth side and a side you pay for, and you can't price both of them in advance from where you're standing. You want the option with no downside. That option isn't in the list. It has never been in the list.

Which means the energy going into which one is possibly not the most important energy in the room. Which one you pick matters less than how you walk after picking. The walking takes up far more days.

* * *

And there's a detail people forget: choosing wrong is usually survivable. You can turn around. You can quit the new job, or move back, or admit in a year that the spreadsheet was optimistic. Almost nobody's life ends because of one wrong turn.

Not choosing is the thing that doesn't turn around. You stay, and it looks like the safe option, and every month the cost of staying is paid quietly, in units too small to notice one at a time — a skill you didn't build, a year of your thirties you didn't spend the way you wanted, a version of yourself you keep postponing.

* * *

Some people, after a while, stop repeating should I go and ask two questions that sit closer to the body.

If I stay exactly like this, for another two years, am I willing?

If I leave now, can I carry what comes after?

Those two don't produce a plan. They produce a rough direction, which is all anyone gets. And the work that remains isn't really the choosing anymore. It's whether, once you've chosen, you'll keep turning around to check the other road.

* * *

Nobody walks a path by stepping perfectly. You pick one, and then you walk it into some kind of shape, over a long time, mostly by continuing.

---

**A question to sit with:** What keeps a person from deciding — that there is no better option, or that they don't want to be responsible for any option at all?
