> A monk asked the master: “What is the ultimate teaching?”
>
> The master said: “Have you had your porridge?”
>
> “Yes.”
>
> “Then go wash your bowl.”

These few lines are often held up as a picture of what Chan looks like. The monk asks for the highest principle. The master does not reach for a scripture. He asks whether the porridge has been eaten, and then tells him to wash the bowl.

Read that way, the Way does not live only in the monastery, on the mountain, or during sitting. Eat when eating. Wash when washing. One thing at a time, and the person is inside it.

* * *

Other paths tend to cut the day in two: here are the pure places, hours, and methods; there is ordinary life, and the line between them is clear. Chan does not divide it that way. It seems to recognize only this place, this moment, this thing in your hands. Whether you stay attentive or drift off, both are part of the same thing.

Because of that, this practice is both easy and wearing.

Easy, because you do not have to acquire anything. No monastery, no robe, no certificate from a teacher. You can start at any moment. Hard, because the more ordinary the moment, the more it asks you to put your attention on what you are actually doing, without fudging it. That is more demanding than assembling the equipment.

> Walk when walking, sit when sitting. Above all, do not wobble. — Pang the Layman

Pang's line sounds like household advice: if you walk, walk; if you sit, sit; do not do one while leaning into the other. Just walk.

But when the walking happens, the mind usually has another room open: the meeting later, the bad moment this morning, the email still unanswered. One after another. Walking means that while you walk, you still know you are walking. The sentence looks light. There is no bottom to it.

* * *

If practice seeps into eating, walking, talking, listening to someone talk — if it is no longer only the thirty minutes set aside in the morning — then the border between practice and the rest of the day stops being clear. The person sitting and the world outside were once two sides. Here they come back together, and what is left is the thing itself, seen plainly.

Living this way is not adding a layer on top of your life. The day stays the same. You are the one who is awake in it, and the light is on.
