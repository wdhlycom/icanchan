At the temple gate the line moves slowly. When your turn comes you lift the incense above your head, close your eyes, and go through the family list: the promotion, the money, the exam your son is sitting in June, your mother's knee.

Someone watching this might smile and call it superstition with better lighting.

But where exactly the line falls between faith and superstition is harder to state than it looks.

![Faith and Superstition](/illustrations/ill-067.jpg)
Chan offers a ruler, and it is not complicated.

Take two people believing the same thing. If the belief slowly makes them clearer, able to make their own decisions, less dependent on something outside to hold them up, more able to stand on their own — that is on the faith side of the line.

If the more they believe, the less they can do without it, the more frightened they become, always looking for a shortcut, unable to face trouble themselves — that is closer to superstition.

* * *

So the difference is not which Buddha, or which doctrine. It is how a person believes.

Two people bow at the same altar. One is asking: Bodhisattva, handle this for me. The Buddha becomes a counterparty, and the transaction is set out plainly — incense on this side, a wish on that side, terms agreed. After bowing, that person does not walk out straighter. They walk out bent a little lower than they came in.

The other bows to remind themselves. They would like to see their own trouble as clearly as the figure on the altar apparently did, to loosen their grip on the things they hold so tightly, to be a bit more patient with the people they live with. For them the Buddha is not a god who carries the problem away. It is someone who walked the road first and pointed. Believing that way, they seem less dependent, not more.

* * *

Chan has its extremes. Danxia burned a wooden Buddha for warmth. Deshan was famous for cursing them. Heard for the first time, this sounds like disrespect. It is aimed somewhere else: at the assumption that the Buddha sits outside you and can settle your difficulties one by one, provided you ask in the right way.

Because the line Chan keeps repeating is this: the Buddha is not outside. It is in the mind. Go looking outside and what you bring back is more reliance. Turn around and look at yourself, and a road begins to show under your feet.

* * *

So the line is not that hard to find. Watch where a belief is taking the person. Toward letting someone else decide, and it slides into superstition. Toward picking the thing up themselves, and it looks more like faith.

Incense or no incense is not the main question. The main question is what is in the heart during that moment of lighting and bowing: carry this for me, or a quiet vow that I would like to become like that.

---

**A question to sit with:** The thing you believe now — is it helping you stand up, or making you more eager to kneel and ask?
