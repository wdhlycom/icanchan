It usually comes at night. You are lying in the dark and the thought arrives on its own: one day I will not be here. The world will keep turning. The sun will come up. There will simply be no me in it.

Your chest goes down. You turn over. Sleep takes a long time. In the morning the thought is gone, and you get dressed and go to work, and it is strange how completely a thing like that can be put away.

![The Fear of Dying](/illustrations/ill-127.jpg)
Being afraid of this is thoroughly ordinary. Everything alive is in the same arrangement. It is not weakness, and it is not morbid thinking. You do not have to be brave about it in front of anyone. On top of the fear, people usually add a second layer of discomfort: why am I such a coward, I should not keep thinking about this. The harder you push it down, the more often it surfaces.

* * *

Chan doesn't go around death. The Buddha left home because old age, sickness and death were pressing on him, and birth and death have sat at the center of this tradition ever since. It is not a forbidden subject here.

But when looking at the fear, there is another question worth asking. Are you afraid of death itself, or are you afraid that when the day comes, the life you meant to live will not have been lived?

* * *

Mostly it is the second one. A person who has done what they wanted to do, seen the people they wanted to see, settled what was on their mind tends to meet the end more easily. What panics people is often not that life ends, but that it ends with a stack of things still unfinished. The trip that kept being moved to next spring. The apology never made. The work left half done in a drawer.

* * *

Seen that way, the fear is not wasted. It taps you on the shoulder now and then: the days are limited; the things you keep setting aside should not be set aside forever.

It isn't a push to spend wildly. It is a push to do — the sentence never said, the visit never made, the thing never started, while there is still time. Nothing dramatic is required. The thing only has to move off the list of later and onto some actual day.

* * *

There is no need to fight the fear, or to crush it in order to be someone calmer. Take it as a prompt. Look at what you have left unlived, then go on with your days. The fear can come back next month and you can ask it the same question again; it does not have to be resolved before you are allowed to live.

---

**A question to sit with:** If the days really came down to one year, which of the things you have never done would you regret?
