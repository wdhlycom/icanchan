Shenxiu and Huineng each wrote a verse, and they are usually told together. It starts from a very homely image: if the mind is a mirror, who is wiping it? Huineng's answer comes down to this — the mirror may never have been dirty, and wiping it every day may be adding one more thing.

* * *

Shenxiu, the senior monk, wrote first. He compared the mind to a mirror stand: a mirror collects dust, so it must be wiped constantly. On that view, practice resembles cleaning. Skip a day and something seems to settle on the mind again. It is a reasonable picture. Most people hear it and think: of course, that is how it must be.

Huineng's verse does not continue the image. It sets the image aside:

> Originally there is not a single thing —<br>
> where could dust alight?

On this reading, the mind is not a mirror stand, an object sitting somewhere that collects dust. There may not even be a stand. And then the question of where the dust lands no longer connects.

* * *

What Huineng was pointing at has a distant echo in some later work on the brain: consciousness does not seem to be an object you can set out on a table, weigh, and point to. When a person sits quietly and looks inward for the “I” that decides, it is usually not there. What is there is one thought after another. The brain keeps reading signals it produced itself, and that process does not stop. Seen this way, the “I” that seems to stand to one side and observe is not located anywhere else. It is inside the activity.

> Looking for the mind is like trying to bite your own teeth. — Mazu Daoyi

It sounds like a mystical remark, but anyone who has sat for a while has probably met it. You watch a thought, you look for the one who is thinking, and what you find is only the thinking. The moment “I am the one watching” appears, that sentence has become one more thing being watched.

* * *

Why go around it this way? Because a lot of our pain starts by assuming a solid “I” is standing there. Someone speaks sharply, someone goes cold, someone comes out ahead, and it feels as if that “I” took the blow.

The looking practiced in Chan is less about argument than about sitting and seeing, bit by bit. After a while, the outline of the one who felt wronged is not as clear as it was.

---

**When you snap at someone and the word “I” comes out, is that “I” the same thing as the anger that was speaking?**
