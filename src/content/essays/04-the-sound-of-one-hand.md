> A monk asked Hakuin: “What is the sound of one hand?”

Hakuin Ekaku had one question that stopped the people who came to practice with him.

It is widely quoted, and most people who have met it remember it. It does not sit there waiting to be answered. It looks more like it is leading you onto a road that does not go through, and after a while the mind has nowhere to put its strength.

* * *

This kind of thing has a name: *gong'an* in Chinese, *koan* in Japanese. The old conversations were written down and kept for later people to work on. The sound of one hand is the most famous of them, but thousands accumulated, and the pattern is much the same: take something the mind cannot close, put it in front of a person, and let them deal with it.

The mind will not sit idle. It puts a meaning on everything. A cloud goes past and it supplies a word. A feeling arrives and it files it, attaches a cause and an effect, and tells the whole thing as a story it can understand.

Most of the time this works well. With a koan it jams.

How does one hand clap? The premise has already removed the clap. You want to solve it and there is nowhere to begin. The mind lunges, grabs air. Lunges again, grabs air. After a few rounds it stops trying.

* * *

Running out is exactly what the question is for.

Not giving up in a sulk. More like this: after thinking for a long time, there is genuinely nowhere left to think, and the effort that always wants a formulation goes slack. When it goes slack, you may not be able to say what happened. The chatter of thoughts gets quieter, and something that was covered becomes audible.

> Great doubt: a ball of fire in the chest. — Hakuin Ekaku

Hakuin called this *great doubt*. Doubt here does not mean indecision or uncertainty. It is closer to the physical sensation of hitting your own limit: the competence you use to find your way, tell things apart, and keep the day continuous suddenly does not work on this one sentence. When you recognize that, something in you loosens, and you cannot say which part moved.

* * *

A word about how these cases became books, because that part is interesting.

In the Song dynasty, Yuanwu Keqin had the old cases collected. He chose a hundred, and under each one wrote a comment and a verse. The book is the *Blue Cliff Record*. A little earlier, Wumen Huikai did the same thing with a hundred cases, adding a short piece of his own under each. That book is the *Gateless Gate*.

So the school that claimed to stand on no words at all left behind more words than almost anyone. Open either collection and under each case you find a monk who did not write, writing a full paragraph to explain it.

* * *

Which is why a koan is not a riddle and has no answer at the back. What loosens first is usually the person who has been trying to solve it.

* * *

I read this case for years. Every time, I only felt that the words “where does the sound come from” were mysterious, and nothing more.

Then one day I noticed something else. In the whole case, nobody ever explains anything. The monk asks, the teacher answers, and it is over. Nobody adds a line about what the sentence really means.

Did you notice that? After “what is the sound of one hand,” there is no more talk.

Before that line, the two of them were still speaking. After it, the momentum to produce an explanation jams.

You cannot prove to another person that they have understood. You can only wait until they stop picking it up again.

* * *

If you want to try this, you do not need to enter a retreat.

Is there something today that has you stuck? A question that keeps offering only yes or no as its exits? Take one. Do not rush to answer it. Let the mind lunge and miss a few times.

I guarantee it will annoy you. Somewhere in the annoyance, it stops ringing.
