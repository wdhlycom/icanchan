The doctor says a word you didn't expect, and the room gets smaller. Or it's nothing that dramatic — a fever that won't break, four days on the couch, the to-do list going stale on the fridge. Either way, your first reaction is fear, and then a scramble to rearrange everything you had planned.

The waiting room has its own atmosphere. Bad chairs, a muted television, everyone holding a folder. You sit there doing arithmetic on your calendar, deciding which calls can be moved and which can't, and it occurs to you that you have been treating your body like a reliable employee who will not suddenly hand in a notice.

What can Chan possibly do here? It's a fair question, and I don't have a clean answer.

![When You're Ill](/illustrations/ill-077.jpg)
Let's be plain about it. Chan does not cure anything and does not remove pain. See the doctor. Take the pills. There is no conflict between that and practice. Chan works on the mind; it is not medicine for the body.

What it can do is narrower and more useful: while you're sick, don't stack fear and grievance on top of the illness.

* * *

Being ill is already wearing. Then we add to it. We worry it will get worse. We resent our bad luck. We replay the weeks before, hunting for what we ignored. We get angry at the unfairness of it. Those thoughts circle, and each lap takes something out of you.

The Buddha made a distinction between the pain of the body and the pain of the mind. The body's pain is not negotiable. But the mind's suffering — built from fear, resentment, refusal — is mostly added on afterward.

* * *

If you can see those two layers, the second one has a chance to loosen.

When it hurts, don't argue with the pain and don't argue with *why me*. Stay with it. Feel what it actually is. The pain is still there, but the fear of being crushed by it gets a little lighter.

* * *

Illness also has a side effect nobody asks for: it forces you to stop. You run until you can't, and then you lie down, and for the first time in months there is nothing to do but notice your own mind. Plenty of people work something out in a sick week that they couldn't work out in a healthy year.

* * *

Illness is bitter. There's no need to dress it up as a gift. But inside the bitterness you can be less frantic, and you can keep a little awareness watching what's happening. For someone lying in that bed, that small amount of awareness may be the most solid thing within reach.

---

**A question to sit with:** Of the suffering in being ill, how much is the body's, and how much has been added by fear and resentment?
