It's 9:40 and the work is basically done. Four tickets closed, everything that came in has been answered, the last document is saved. Nothing left needs you. You stay anyway, and open the inbox again. Then tomorrow's calendar. Then the inbox.

Nothing is exactly wrong. That's the strange part. Things are getting done, plenty of them, and you still cannot locate the feeling of having done enough. There is a voice with a very reasonable tone asking whether you pushed hard enough today.

The usual response is to push harder. Add half an hour. Take on the thing nobody asked you to take on. But this is a treadmill, and the speed doesn't change the view. The extra thirty minutes doesn't loosen anything in your chest. It usually tightens it, because now there is a new baseline.

Look closely at the panic and it isn't really about volume. It's about bookkeeping. Somewhere before each task, a small internal clerk writes it down: this one has to turn into something.

![The Harder You Push](/illustrations/ill-081.jpg)
Once the ledger is open, the work stops being entirely yours. A raise needs someone else's signature. A promotion needs a room full of people to agree. Doing something well needs someone to notice it was done well. The share of the day you actually control gets smaller, and the less you control, the less safe it feels to stop.

Baizhang Huaihai is remembered for a line: a day without work is a day without eating. He was famously literal about it. He worked well into old age, and when his monks hid his tools so he would rest, he refused to eat. What is interesting isn't the austerity. It's that when he worked, he seems to have just worked. There is no story of him hoeing a field while calculating the harvest, or while wondering how the monks were judging his technique.

The point isn't to do less. It's that the rope tied around the outcome can be given a little slack. People hear this and jump to some version of lying down, which misses it by a wide margin. The work still gets done. You just stop carrying each task to the counter to see what it will buy.

* * *

There is a useful split here, and it isn't complicated. Whether you did a thing properly — you know that, roughly, from the inside. Whether it succeeds — that depends on timing, other people, luck, a hiring freeze in another department. One of those is yours. The other was never yours.

Keep them fused and no amount of rest works. You leave at six, get home, and the ledger is still open on the kitchen table.

Let them come apart and you can work until ten and still put the laptop down without the chest tightening, because the ten o'clock was not a down payment on anything.

---

**A question to sit with:** How much of that inability to stop is the work being urgent, and how much is stopping being the thing you can't stand?
