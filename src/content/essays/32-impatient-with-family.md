Your patience at work is reliable. You can stay calm with a colleague who repeats himself, smile through a client call that should have ended twenty minutes ago, say excuse me to a stranger who steps on your foot on the train.

Then you come through your own front door. Your father asks, mildly, why you are still up, and something in your voice comes out sharp. Your partner says one sentence slightly wrong and your face closes before you have decided to close it.

You wonder about this afterward. Of all the people in your life, why are these the ones who get the worst of you?

![Short With the People Closest](/illustrations/ill-115.jpg)
A lot of it has to do with how easily you let go at home. Outside, you are holding yourself a certain way. You are aware that a harsh sentence has consequences, that a client can leave, that a colleague will remember. At home the holding stops. These people will not walk out over one bad tone, you think, and so the impatience goes out unedited.

The care itself has not faded. It has just become as unremarkable as eating and sleeping, something you no longer stop to notice. It is the same ease that makes a house feel like a house, and the same ease that lets the guard come down without anyone deciding to lower it.

* * *

Chan talks about treating close and distant alike. That is not an instruction to be as polite to your mother as you are to a delivery driver — that would make the house stranger, not warmer. It points at something smaller: the closer someone is, the easier it is to skip the small effort of speaking to them well.

People outside your door you see once and maybe never again. The people inside are no more guaranteed, but they are there every day, so it feels as though there will always be time.

* * *

The moment impatience rises is a useful moment, if you can catch it. The sentence is already at the tip of your tongue; let it sit there half a second longer. The face that was about to close; let it loosen. In that small pause you stop being dragged along by the feeling and become someone standing beside it, watching it go by.

Nobody is asking you to be pleasant all day. That kind of evenness, held by force, is its own performance. It is more like this: once in a while, before the words go out, you remember that the person in front of you is probably the last one who should get your worst.

* * *

Some people, the next time the heat comes up, run a quick test first. If this were a client, would I say it this way?

If the answer is no, then why does it come out so easily here?

---

**A question to sit with:** Do we keep our good temper for people who hardly matter, and hand our worst to the people we care about most?
