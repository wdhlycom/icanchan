The argument ended hours ago. You are in bed now, and the film is still running.

You replay it. The line he said, the line you said, and the anger comes back fresher than it was at the time. Then you run it again with a better script, one where you take him apart so completely that he has nothing left to say. Then a third time, rehearsing what you will do the next time he dares to open his mouth.

Somewhere in the third version you notice your jaw is set and your shoulders have climbed toward your ears, as if the other person were still in the room. They are not. They are asleep, or out, or watching something on television, entirely unbothered by a conversation you are having alone in the dark.

The night passes. You did not win the argument. You are just worn out.

![Replaying the Argument](/illustrations/ill-071.jpg)
Psychology has a name for this: rumination, from the way cattle bring their food back up and chew it again. The cow chews grass. You chew a conversation that has already happened and cannot be edited.

Chan looks at it from a fairly blunt angle. The problem, it suggests, is not that you are thinking. It is that you did not notice you were thinking.

* * *

What makes rumination nasty is how quietly it starts. You believe you are getting the thing straight, laying it out, finally understanding it. In fact you are being bitten, a little at a time. The anger rises, the anger feeds the replay, the replay feeds the anger, and the circle closes.

What Chan offers is not the instruction to stop thinking. The harder you try to stop, the less it works. What it offers is a little room for seeing: to recognize, while it is happening, that this is a replay.

* * *

Being inside the film and seeing the film are not the same. Inside, you are an actor. You get angry on cue, then hurt, then sorry for yourself, then angry again. Seeing, you have moved to a seat in the audience. You know that what is on the screen is something your own mind is projecting.

Whether the film stops is not really the point. Knowing that it is a film, and that it is not you, seems to be enough.

* * *

Some people, when the replay starts up, say quietly to themselves: it is playing again.

No verdict on it, no attempt to shove it away. Just the recognition. Each recognition puts a little distance between you and the screen. Do it often enough and the volume comes down by itself.

---

**A question to sit with:** When you replay an argument, are you trying to understand it, or to win it?
