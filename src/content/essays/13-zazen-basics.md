Plenty of people have thought about sitting meditation. Few have sat down.

Ask what stopped them and the answers are similar. Not sure about the posture. Afraid of not lasting ten minutes. Afraid of wasting the time. Afraid of not being the type.

Most of that happens before the chair. Once you are actually seated, most of it scatters.

![Ten Minutes to Start Sitting](/illustrations/ill-119.jpg)
You do not need a quiet room first, or a cushion, or a set of robes. A chair, ten minutes, and one concrete thing to do is enough.

The posture is not mysterious. Sit upright, do not slump, do not stiffen, rest your hands on your thighs. Comfortable is enough. Lotus is optional.

Eyes closed or half open, then count the breath. Inhale, exhale, one. Inhale, exhale, two. Up to ten, then start again at one.

That is the whole instruction. The one thing to notice: when you realize the count is gone, begin again from one.

Nothing here is athletic. The count is not a score, and nobody is checking whether you reached ten.

There is a view built into that. It does not expect you to stay on the breath. It assumes you will drift. Halfway to ten, your mind is at tomorrow's meeting or back inside something someone said. That is ordinary. It is not a failed sit. The failure would be not noticing at all, and that is not something you can arrange on purpose.

* * *

Many people hear sitting as emptying the mind. That is off by a wide margin. The mind does not empty, and it does not need to. What gets trained is the noticing, the small note that says *ah, I drifted*.

Each noticing is one return from being dragged along by a thought. Ten minutes, however many returns, is ten minutes of that. Drifting is not the opponent. Noticing the drift is the skill being worn smooth. You do not get better at never wandering. You get a little quicker at coming back, and less annoyed at yourself when you do.

* * *

Someone pulls a chair over before bed, sits straight, and counts breaths for ten minutes. No target, no chasing a special feeling. Counting, and coming back.

After a few nights of it, most people get one thing from the experiment: sitting was always available. They simply had not moved. The cushion never asked for a better version of them. It only asked for ten minutes.

If ten feels long, sit for five. If five feels long, follow three breaths and stand up. The length is not the point. The point is that the same ten minutes will be there tomorrow.

**A question to sit with:** What has kept you from starting, not knowing the method, or the prospect of meeting a quieter and less familiar version of yourself?
