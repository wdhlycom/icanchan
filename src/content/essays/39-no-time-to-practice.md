The alarm goes at 6:50. By 7:05 there are eleven messages. The day is four meetings deep before you have eaten anything that counts as lunch, and by the time you get home you're horizontal on the couch with your shoes still on and the phone face-down on your chest.

Forget sitting meditation. Getting a glass of water means walking to the kitchen, which is currently three rooms too far.

And that's when the thought arrives, mild and reasonable: maybe I'm just not able to practice right now. Maybe this is a season where that isn't available to me.

![Too Busy to Practice](/illustrations/ill-083.jpg)
Behind that thought is an assumption worth pulling out and looking at. It is that practice is an activity that needs its own slot. A clear hour. A quiet room. A cushion, maybe a candle, definitely no notifications.

If that's the definition, then busyness is a wall, the wall is very tall, and you are on the wrong side of it.

Huineng's line — walking, standing, sitting, lying down, all of it is chan — is not a metaphor. It isn't poetry pointing at something loftier. It means what it says: none of those positions is outside. Nothing gets excluded. And when the old teachers were saying this, the monastery still had water to haul, wood to split, guests to receive, and a schedule that started before light.

* * *

So the question isn't whether you have time. It's where the mind is while you're busy.

Watch two people in the same meeting. The first is listening, but not really — a third of them is already drafting a reply to the email that just came in, and another third is worrying about the thing due at five. Their body is in the room. The task in front of them is unfinished and the next three are already crowding in. By evening they have been dragged through the day by their own momentum.

The second is equally busy. Same calendar. But they are doing one thing, and then the next. The hurry is still there — nobody is pretending it isn't — but the thing in their hands doesn't get dropped every ninety seconds.

The two days look identical on a calendar. They are not the same day.

* * *

Which means not sitting isn't automatically a failure. Smaller versions are available, and they cost nothing extra.

In the meeting, let the person finish their sentence before you start assembling the correction. While pushing the proposal out, finish the paragraph before you start telling yourself you're late. At dinner, eat the bite that's on the fork before you swallow the next one along with whatever is on the screen.

None of that requires a room, a cushion, or an extra twenty minutes. It requires remembering, which is the hard part.

* * *

The practice isn't carving out a session. It's spending the minutes you are already spending with a little less of yourself somewhere else.

So the next time you can't breathe, don't wait for after this crunch. That clause has a way of renewing itself. Stop for one breath. Put your attention back on the single thing in front of you. That's the whole instruction, and it is enough for the next hour.

---

**A question to sit with:** Is being busy an obstacle to practice, or is it the most honest test of it?
