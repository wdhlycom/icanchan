> The Cloth Sack monk carried a cloth bag on a staff wherever he went. Everything he owned was inside it.
>
> A monk asked him, “What is the great meaning of the Buddha's teaching?”
>
> He set the bag down and stood there with his hands folded.
>
> The monk asked, “Is there anything higher than that?”
>
> He picked the bag up and walked away.

* * *

The first question, he puts the bag down. That gesture is not hard to understand. A load carried all day is set on the ground, and the person loosens.

The second question is more interesting. “Anything higher” means: after putting it down, is there a higher, cleaner, more final place to go? The moment a person lets go of the load on his back, he is likely to turn and grab something else. Even the sentence “I have let go” can be picked up and held.

Budai does not explain. He puts the bag back on his shoulder and walks.

* * *

Letting go does not seem to be a finish line, and it is not a certificate you can hang on the wall.

Some people keep saying “I am not competing anymore.” Said often enough, the sentence itself becomes a thing to protect, and they will not let anyone touch it. Others quit a busy life, and then spend their free days checking whether the free time is clean enough, whether anything else has leaked into it. They end up more tired than before.

The bag is the same bag. The same food and clothing are inside it and nothing has been removed. It looks like the same load. The person carrying it is a little different. Before, he carried it and felt he had to give an account of it. Later, he carries it and mostly that is all: he carries it, and walks the way he was going.

He does not stay where he set it down. He stands, picks the bag back up, and continues on his road. People who tell this story often remember only the first letting go. The rest is easy to miss.

**Is there something you said you put down long ago, which is still in your hand under a nicer name?**
