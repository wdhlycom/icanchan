The sentence that surfaces most often is some version of *I am not good enough*. It doesn't arrive like a passing thought. It arrives like a finding. A voice inside has already read the verdict aloud, and now it waits for you to accept it.

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, usually shortened to ACT, says something plain about this. A thought, in the end, is a thought. The therapists have a name for the move that follows: cognitive defusion. The point is not to erase the sentence but to put a little distance between the person and the sentence. The thought is still there. The equals sign is gone.

---

## Letting go is not eviction

Chan uses different vocabulary for roughly the same move. Letting go.

Letting go is not an eviction, and it is not pretending the thought never came. It is closer to seeing that what drifted past was one cloud, and one cloud cannot hold up the whole sky. The thought says *I am not good enough*. You hear it. You don't follow it out the door, and you don't turn around to wrestle it.

Why wrestling usually fails is worth spelling out. Suppose you decide to refute the sentence. You gather ten pieces of evidence that you are, in fact, good enough. Two days later the sentence is back, often unchanged. It grows out of a deeper set of mental habits, and it was never well anchored to the facts in front of you. The more seriously you argue, the more it looks as if you have conceded that it deserves the effort.

This is the trap: the rebuttal keeps the sentence in the room.

Chan and ACT point to a cheaper route. Don't specially welcome it. Don't hard push it back. Keep doing the thing that is in front of you.

Defusion is sometimes sold as positive thinking, which it isn't. Swapping *I am not good enough* for *I am enough* leaves the machinery untouched: a sentence is still being treated as a verdict, only now the verdict is flattering. ACT's claim is structural. A thought is an event in a mind, not a description of the world, and an event can be observed without being obeyed.

---

Here is where the distinction shows up in ordinary hours. A person who takes the sentence as fact declines the invitation, skips the application, says nothing in the meeting. Not because the facts changed. Because a thought was read as a report. The loss is quiet and mostly invisible, which is why it can go on for years.

The defusion exercise, if you try it, is unimpressive to watch. The sentence arrives — *I am not good enough* — and instead of arguing, you note that a thought arrived and that it was that one. It seems too small to matter. The smallness is the point. You are not trying to replace the sentence with a better one. You are trying to stop the automatic promotion from thought to fact.

At three in the morning the same exercise is harder. The sentence brings a list. The list looks like evidence, and by four you are conducting a trial in which you are the defendant. The hour makes the list look impartial. It helps to notice that the list is assembled by the same mind that produced the sentence. That doesn't make the list a lie. It makes it a brief, not a ruling.

None of this requires the sentence to go quiet. It may keep arriving for years; in my own case it has. What changes is what happens after it arrives. You hear it, you don't argue, you don't obey, and you go back to the email, the dish, the phone call.

Chan's old instruction is not to fight the thought but to see it for what it is. A cloud is not an argument against the sky. It is weather. Weather passes through, and the sky is not improved or damaged by the passing.

---

A thought is not a fact. The sentence is easy to agree with and hard to use. Using it is one move, repeated: hear the sentence, notice that it is a thought, carry on with what you were doing. Nothing dramatic follows. A little distance opens, about the width of a breath, and the sentence goes back to being what it was before you promoted it.
