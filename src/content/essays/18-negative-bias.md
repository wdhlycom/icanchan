A single remark can sit in the chest for a whole day. It was not even a cruel remark. You turn it over at lunch, on the walk home, at the sink. Ten compliments from the same week are gone; you couldn't retrieve one if you tried.

This isn't a personality flaw. The mind leans that way by construction.

Psychology calls it the negativity bias. Bad news, threats, and criticism draw a larger response than good news does, and the response lasts longer. A criticism lands and stays. Praise is gone by the time you turn around.

It isn't hard to see why. For most of the long human past, missing a piece of fruit cost you a meal. Missing a predator cost you everything. A mind that recorded the bad things a little more heavily, that refused to let a danger slide past, was more likely to leave descendants. The trade was worth making. We are still running it, mostly in rooms where the predators are social.

Buddhist texts have a word, dukkha, usually translated as suffering. Readers often hear a verdict in it: life is pain, accept that and carry on. Looked at closely, the word is doing something narrower. It describes the mind's handling of what it doesn't like. A negative experience arrives, the mind takes hold of it and enlarges it. One thing goes wrong, and the mind lifts it up and plays it again. Whatever went smoothly seems never to have happened. None of this is a decision to brood. The machinery is built to move first.

---

## Seeing the tilt

Seeing the bias clearly is already some help, before you do anything with it.

The usual sequence the next time something goes wrong: the mind issues a verdict about you. I am the sort of person who always expects the worst. But nearly everyone tilts. The difference is degree, not category. The verdict is not evidence that you are unusually gloomy; it is the bias filing a report about itself.

Underneath that is a more useful observation. Once the tilt has been seen, it stops resembling the truth. It looks like a habit with its own motor, one that will surface again tomorrow, probably before breakfast.

The shift is smaller than it sounds. The bias does not pack up and leave. It still rises. You are simply no longer obliged to believe every sentence it hands you.

I watched this at close range with a performance review. Nine lines of praise, one line of doubt. A week later I could recite the doubtful line word for word. The nine had blurred into a general warmth. Nothing about the year had changed except which part of it the mind kept lifting into the light.

Memory doesn't only keep the bad thing; it consults it. Ask yourself how the month went, and the mind reaches for its heaviest file. The answer feels like a summary of the month. It is closer to a summary of the entries that came with weight. A friend asks about the week, and you report the one bad meeting. The report is honest. It is also not the week.

The cost is not dramatic. You make a decision in the mood a criticism left behind. You read a mixed day as a bad one and carry the reading into the week. The old texts put this under dukkha. The modern term is bias. Neither describes a moral failure. Both describe an apparatus that tilts and then reports its tilt as the shape of the world.

---

What can be done about it is smaller than the shelf of books suggests. You won't argue the bias out of the brain, and setting out to become a relentlessly positive person just installs a second distortion with better manners.

The narrower move is available. When the day has been mixed, call it mixed. When the verdict arrives, notice that it arrived. Not that it is false, necessarily. Only that it is a report from a tilted desk.

The bias will still be there tomorrow. It will still show up, and the remark will still sit in the chest longer than the praise. What changes is the signature at the bottom of the page. You don't have to sign every word of it.
