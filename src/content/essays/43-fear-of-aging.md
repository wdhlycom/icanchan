You catch yourself in a shop window and for a second don't recognize the person walking. There are lines at the corners of your eyes that weren't there in the photos from five years ago. One late night now takes three days to recover from. A new colleague calls you ma'am, or sir, and is not being sarcastic.

There are smaller signs too. You scroll further than you used to on a form to find your birth year. The pharmacist hands you the reading glasses without asking. Someone at a party describes a band you loved as music your parents listened to, and they're not wrong.

The thought arrives like a small splinter. *I'm getting old.* It goes in, and then it sits there.

![The Fear of Getting Older](/illustrations/ill-117.jpg)
Out loud, what we name is the surface: wrinkles, aches, the knee that complains on stairs. Look one layer in and it's usually something else. The fear of no longer being needed — at work you used to be the one people came to, and now you're the one they route around. The fear that the road has quietly narrowed. At twenty-five everything was still possible; somewhere along the way the options got used up, and the phone rings less than it did.

The wrinkle is the presentable excuse. The rest is harder to say at a dinner party.

* * *

Chan talks about impermanence, about birth and aging and sickness and death. This is not an instruction to accept aging with a shrug. It just puts the facts on the table: aging cannot be dodged, fear does not slow it, and the only thing left to change is how you keep company with it.

* * *

Aging is loss, and it's dishonest to pretend otherwise. The body is less reliable. Positions get handed to someone younger. Some doors close and stay closed.

It is also a putting down. The status you had to defend, the baggage you'd wanted to drop for years, the long-tightened string of living up to what other people expected. When you were young you were pushed along by obligations and couldn't help it. Older, you might get to live on your own terms for the first time. That is not a consolation prize. It is the actual trade.

* * *

People afraid of aging keep measuring themselves with a young person's ruler. But a life isn't one upward line where the downhill cancels the rest. Each stretch has its own weather. The view at this height is simply not the view from before.

You can't stop the wrinkles. What stays yours to decide is what kind of older person you become: the one who resents everything, or the one who slowly starts to see clearly.

---

**A question to sit with:** Is it aging you fear — or getting old without having lived the life you actually wanted?
