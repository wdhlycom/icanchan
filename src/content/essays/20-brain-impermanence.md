Somewhere in middle age a person begins to believe the shape is set. Temperament, habits, the reaction that arrives before thought — all of it seems settled, and the rest of life looks like a long repetition of it. The old saying puts a seal on the feeling: rivers and mountains are easy to change; a person's nature is not.

There is comfort in that sentence. It ends the conversation. If the shape is fixed, there is nothing to attempt and no failure to explain. I have used it that way myself.

Brain science tells a somewhat different story. The term is neuroplasticity. The connections in the brain are revised by experience, again and again, and the revision does not stop when a person reaches adulthood. A region used often for a particular skill grows a little thicker; a region left alone for a long time thins. Seen that way, the brain is less like an object poured into a mold and set aside, and more like a circuit board that is always being re-laid. The revisions are small at any single moment, which is why the process is easy to doubt.

## The other face of impermanence

Chan has a word for this, and it is usually heard as bad news. Impermanence.

The word is taken to mean that whatever is in your hand will come apart. That part is true. But there is a second face to it: what can come apart can also still change. The habits you have decided are permanent, the wound left alone for years, the thought you have been holding shut — all of them look solid, and none of them is welded in place. They came together out of conditions, not out of cast iron. Conditions gather and scatter; nothing in the arrangement is permanent, including the arrangement.

This is not optimism. It promises nothing about the direction of change. It says only that the door has not been bricked up. A tendency that formed can be worn down. A response that was learned can be learned differently. Whether it will be is another matter, and no old text promises that.

Nothing about this is visible from the outside. The work is dull and repetitive, and the change shows up as a delay rather than an announcement.

Zazen works on whatever this capacity is. Nothing in the instruction is exotic: sit, breathe, return. You sit, you follow the breath, you watch thoughts come and go. Done repeatedly, the old circuit — the one that starts running the moment a thought pulls — is revised. Beside it, a new path is worn, slowly, a few steps at a time. The path is not built by insight; it is built by traffic. The repetitions are not wasted. The brain seems to record every one of them.

I have seen the difference in small things. A reaction that used to arrive before I could speak now arrives with a half-second of space around it. That half-second is not enlightenment. It is a path that has been walked often enough to be visible. Nothing about it was impressive. It was a gap where there used to be none.

---

I don't want to overstate what this means. Nothing here promises that sitting will produce a different person. The claim is smaller and steadier: the material is not fixed, and practice is one way of working it. What the old texts call impermanence and what the labs call plasticity are not the same claim. One describes how things are; the other describes what tissue does. They point the same way, and neither needs the other's permission.

The fixed view also protects itself. If the shape is set, you don't attempt the thing that might have changed it, and the absence of change becomes the proof. I have run that loop in one area of my life for years, and it looked like evidence the whole time.

The old saying about rivers and mountains was probably never meant as a law of nature. It sounds like something said to people who had stopped trying, a description of a mood rather than of the material. The mood is understandable. The material is still moving.
