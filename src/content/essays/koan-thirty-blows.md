> Deshan Xuanjian entered the hall and said to the assembly, “Thirty blows whether you can speak or not.”

* * *

When someone asks you a question, there usually seem to be two roads: speak, or do not speak. You pick one, and once you have picked, the answer is considered delivered.

Deshan's sentence closes both at once.

It may not be impatience. When you speak, what usually comes out is a line selected from the sentences you have stored up. When you refuse to speak, the mind can produce its own small satisfaction: *I have risen above words.* The two look opposite. Underneath they are similar. Both are already thinking about what this answer will look like when it is handed over.

So it does not much matter which side the stick lands on. Neither side is a place to hide.

* * *

Read enough cases where the answer does not match the question, and a thought starts to form: since every answer is wrong, the safe move is to say nothing. Deshan's sentence leaves no room for that bit of cleverness either.

There are plenty of scenes like this in ordinary life. Someone asks you about a difficult matter. Say something offhand and you worry you sound dismissive. Say nothing at all and you worry you look superior. Neither is comfortable, and most of the discomfort comes from the fact that before you open your mouth, you have already run a lap around the question of how to come out of this looking steady.

Once that calculation starts, you are mostly not with the matter anymore. You are going around and around the question of how other people will see your answer.

The thirty blows do not look like punishment for a wrong answer. They seem aimed at the mind that is always looking for a safe place to stand.
