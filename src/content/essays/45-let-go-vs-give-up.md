The email went out on Thursday. By Friday you have rewritten it in your head four times. On Saturday you open the sent folder, then the thread, then the sent folder again. There is nothing to add. There is nothing to fix. The work is done, the next move belongs to someone else, and your hand is still on it.

This is when the phrase let it go shows up, usually in a friend's voice or your own. You say it. It doesn't take.

Because the moment you say it, a question comes up behind it: is this just admitting I lost? And a worse one after that: is this the same as giving up?

So the hand closes tighter. It's exhausting, and the exhaustion is hard to locate, because nothing is actually being done.

![Letting Go or Giving Up](/illustrations/ill-075.jpg)
Chan tends to discuss letting go and giving up in the same breath, which is exactly why the two get confused. One word apart in English, and much further apart in practice.

Giving up says: more effort won't change anything, so I'm stopping. The line underneath is I don't want it anymore. The energy withdraws from the project, and often from the room.

Letting go says: I spent the effort I had. The result no longer gets to drag me around. The line underneath is I did my part; the rest was never mine to hold. The energy doesn't withdraw. It just stops being a leash.

* * *

Here is the picture. Someone is chasing something and getting beaten up by it — a promotion, a book, a company, a person. Turn around, walk away, never mention it again: that is usually giving up. Keep going, but stop requiring that it must work out — glad if it does, able to keep living if it doesn't — that is closer to letting go.

Notice what the first person is doing: leaving, mostly to get away from the feeling. Notice what the second person is not: serene. They are still walking. They still want it. It's just that the rope pulled tight across their chest has gone slack by an inch.

* * *

Chan has surprisingly little to say about stopping. Baizhang's line — a day without work, a day without eating — came from a man who worked hard his whole life, including past the age when he could have stopped. What is asked to be released is not the work. It's the small, constant hope that the result will come out the way you pictured.

Because the result was never in your hands. What was in your hands is how you did the thing. Doing that part properly is the whole assignment. Where the result lands is decided by other people, timing, and weather. Oddly, these aren't two separate projects. When you do your part cleanly and stop gripping the outcome, both tend to happen at once, and they don't fight each other.

* * *

So the next time you are weighing whether to let go, it helps to move the question over a little. What exactly do I want to loosen — the thing itself, or the requirement that it succeed?

If it's the thing itself, that is closer to giving up, and worth a second look before you do it.

If it's only the outcome, and how it looked to other people, then loosening that costs you nothing. You haven't abandoned anything. You've set down the part that was never yours to carry.

---

**A question to sit with:** What's actually being held onto — the thing itself, or the thought that it has to work?
