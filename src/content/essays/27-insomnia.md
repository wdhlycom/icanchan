The room is dark, your body is flat on the mattress, and your mind is going through the day like a man searching drawers.

Something you said in a meeting that came out wrong. An email you still owe someone. A conversation from three years ago that the other person has certainly forgotten. Each item gets a hearing. Each item gets a rebuttal.

Then you check the time. Two. A while later, three-thirty. Then four. Every glance adds a little more pressure. You have to be up in four hours. So why aren't you asleep.

Here is the strange part. The more urgent it gets, the more awake you are.

![Three in the Morning](/illustrations/ill-093.jpg)
What makes a bad night bad is not entirely the not-sleeping. It is the panic that the not-sleeping produces. You are lying there with your eyes shut, and your hands are busy with a job harder than anything you did all day: you are trying to force yourself to sleep, on a deadline, by will.

Sleep does not respond well to being chased. It is closer to a cat. The more directly you reach for it, the further under the sofa it goes.

* * *

Chan does not hand out a mantra that puts you under in three minutes. It does something close to the opposite. It suggests you set down, for now, the thought that you must sleep immediately.

The body is already lying down. That part is finished. Whether sleep arrives is not really yours to decide. What is yours is lying still and not arguing with the night.

* * *

Some people stop pushing and say instead: I will lie here and keep my body company.

Feel your back against the mattress. The weight of the blanket. Your breath going out and coming back. No counting sheep, no rules about technique, no posture to get right. Just move your attention off the question of why you are still awake and set it down, lightly, on the body you are lying in.

Thoughts still come. They do not need to be shown out. They arrive, they leave, and you neither follow the one heading for the door nor pick a fight with the one that stays.

* * *

This is hard, because at three in the morning most of us are used to having something in our hands. Lying still with nothing to do feels unsafe. What Chan seems to allow here is the strange move of letting yourself accomplish nothing for an hour.

A tired body falls asleep on its own. It does not need you to worry it there.

---

**A question to sit with:** On a night like that, what actually hurts — the sleeplessness, or the discovery that you, of all people, cannot sleep?
