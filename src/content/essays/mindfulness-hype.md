The word mindfulness has been doing two jobs for a while now, and only one of them is the job it was built for. In the last decade it moved out of meditation halls and into company trainings, bookshop shelves, and the covers of courses about quitting smoking and losing weight. Hear it often enough and a shape forms in the mind: every difficulty has a more presentable solution, provided you attach the right adjective to the front of it. Mindful eating. Mindful parenting. Mindful email. The word stops pointing at an activity and starts acting like a seal of quality.

It would be unfair to say none of this rests on anything. Part of it does. In the late 1970s Jon Kabat-Zinn started an eight-week course at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center, teaching people to place attention on the breath and on sensations in the body. The course has since been through a large number of randomized controlled trials. Set against people who did nothing, and against people who did relaxation exercises, some of those who finished the eight weeks came out with less anxiety, better sleep, and chronic pain that ground at them somewhat less.

The effects are not large. In medical terms they are moderate. But they reproduce, and reproducibility is what earns a claim. This part can be stated plainly, without hedging and without inflation: for some people, under some conditions, the course helps.

## Past the edge of the evidence

Further out, the language goes slack. Brain imaging is the usual exhibit. Scans show that long-term meditators have thicker gray matter in certain regions. That finding says meditators' brains look like this. It does not say meditation made them this way. People who can sit still and prefer quiet may be the ones who keep at it for years; other factors may be running alongside, and the scan cannot separate them. By the time the result reaches a headline it reads as meditation rewires the brain, and the reader crosses from correlation to cause without feeling the step.

Weight loss, quitting smoking, job performance — each has studies behind it. The samples are small, the conclusions disagree with one another, and a different team running the same protocol often fails to get the earlier result. That is not proof the thing does nothing. It is proof that nobody yet knows. Yet the course page and the book cover state these claims as firmly as they state the eight-week stress program, in the same typeface, with the same confidence. A reader has no way to see which sentence has a floor under it and which one is hanging in the air.

It is not hard to understand why the packaging works. Most people want a method that is both easy and effective, and mindfulness fits the outline of that want: no medication, no belief required, a few minutes a day. Anyone who has actually practiced knows the outline is wrong. You sit down, attention wanders, you notice, you bring it back, it wanders again, you bring it back again. That loop is the whole exercise. It is tedious, and it is slow, and the improvement, when it comes, is the kind you notice in retrospect over months rather than in a week.

So a practice whose entire logic is not rushing toward a result gets sold as something that works in days. There is a small absurdity in that. It would stay small if it stopped there.

## Who ends up paying

The people who end up worse off are the ones the promise was aimed at. Someone caught in anxiety reads that a few weeks of practice will change things, buys the course, sits down every morning, and at week five is as anxious as before. From inside that experience, the reasonable conclusion is that they did it wrong — that the fault is in their discipline, their posture, their inability to concentrate. So they stop. Something that would have helped a little gets put down and does not get picked up again.

That is the real cost of the overreach. Not that the claims are false in every case, but that the exaggeration spends the credibility of the part that was true. A person who has been let down once by a confident label is unlikely to distinguish, later, between the claim that failed and the claim that would have held.

I do not think the answer is to stop saying mindfulness helps. It does, inside limits that can be named. The eight-week course has earned a claim of moderate relief for some people with anxiety, with sleep trouble, with chronic pain. That is a real thing to offer someone, and it is more than most interventions can say. Everything else is a much thinner file. Presenting a thin file at the same weight as a thick one is how a whole category loses its standing, including the portion that deserved to keep it.

The distinction is not complicated, and it does not require hostility to the practice. It requires only that the sentence on the cover match the sentence the research supports. The course was designed to help people suffer a little less. It does that, for some. It was never designed to fix everything, and the fact that it is now sold that way says more about the market for easy answers than about what happens when you sit down and watch your breath.
