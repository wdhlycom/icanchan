You eat with your phone propped against the salt shaker. You walk to the station with a podcast in your ears. You shower with a speaker on the shelf. The small gaps in the day — the walk to the train, the ten minutes waiting for water to boil, the meal alone at the kitchen counter — all get filled.

Fill enough of them and the phrase being present starts to sound like a rumor. You are busy from seven in the morning until eleven at night, and there may not be one moment in the whole day when the entire person is in the same room as the body.

![Eating With Your Phone](/illustrations/ill-107.jpg)
Some people decide that presence requires a separate appointment. You set aside twenty minutes, sit cross-legged on a cushion, and practice. Three days later the cushion is in the closet and the explanation is that work got busy.

This is mostly a misunderstanding. The present is not a block of time you have to clear in your calendar. It is the second you are already in. Practicing it is not one more item added to the day. It is doing, with your attention, the thing your hands are already doing.

* * *

When you eat, eat. When you walk, walk. This does not mean no other thoughts are allowed at the table. Thoughts wander. Everybody's do. What matters is noticing that yours has wandered and bringing it back.

Notice, bring it back. Five minutes later it wanders again, and you bring it back again. That is the whole exercise, repeated. There is no more advanced version of it.

* * *

Concretely, some people start here.

At a meal, put the phone on the far side of the room. Take a bite. Taste it. Notice whether it is hot, salty, soft, whether you are chewing fast. When you drift off into tomorrow's meeting, come back and take another bite. Most people find they cannot remember eating the second half of the plate.

On a walk, take the earbuds out. Feel the sole of your foot land. Listen to the sounds that were there before you put headphones on. Look at the people coming the other way. When you drift, come back and look again.

Nobody manages this from beginning to end, and that is not the standard. What you are practicing is the return, and the return gets quicker with use.

* * *

Seen this way, presence is less a goal you reach than a skill that grows. What improves is not how tightly you hold your attention. It is how soon you notice you have let go of it.

Do that enough times, and you start waking up in the middle of your own distraction a little earlier.
