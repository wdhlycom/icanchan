A friend turned thirty-six last month. Good dinner, good cake, a table of people who liked her. Somewhere between the second glass and the last slice she said, half joking, that she had finally collected everything she spent fifteen years wanting: the apartment, the car, a job with her name on an office door, a household that mostly ran.

Then she said the other thing. That some nights, lying awake, a sentence comes up out of nowhere. *And then what?* All those years, for this? The empty spot hasn't moved.

The strange part is that nothing is wrong. The fridge hums. The neighbors' TV goes quiet at eleven. By every measure she drew up at twenty-five, she has arrived. And the arriving feels like standing in a hallway, waiting for a door to open into the rest of it.

![Everything, and Still Unhappy](/illustrations/ill-079.jpg)
Unhappiness doesn't always mean something is missing. Often you only find out after you have the thing that it doesn't fill the hole. The hole was never shaped like a car.

It would be easy to blame having. But the trouble started earlier, in the aim. We assume happiness is stored in objects, so we keep reaching outward. We get one, feel good for a season, and then it stops being enough. Happiness is less like an acquisition and more like a loosening somewhere behind the ribs.

* * *

The Buddha named this the suffering of not getting what you seek. The seeking itself aches. Getting it buys a short high, then a new want surfaces and the ache resumes. It is like chasing your own shadow down a street: always one step ahead, never catchable.

The problem isn't the thing you want. It's the arrangement you've made with it — that once it arrives, the emptiness will be settled. That road does not go there.

* * *

So where is it? Chan doesn't hurry to answer, and it won't hand you a slogan. It asks you to stop for a minute and look at the unhappiness directly. What, exactly, is missing?

Usually not a specific item. Usually it's the ability to keep company with the day you actually have. To stay steady when things are busy and to be good company for yourself when they're not. To hold what's in your hands and not panic over what isn't.

That sounds small, and it isn't. Most of us can be busy without being frantic, or idle without reaching for the phone, for about four minutes.

* * *

None of that can be handed over. It has to be practiced, slowly, the way you'd learn an instrument. What you're practicing is a mind that can be settled without the external furniture.

Keep the apartment. Keep chasing the next thing if you want. Just stop loading the whole weight of your happiness onto them. They were never built to carry it.

---

**A question to sit with:** The thing you are working hardest for right now — when it is finally in your hands, will the empty place go away?
