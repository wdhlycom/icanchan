> A monk asked Zhaozhou, “Does a dog have Buddha nature or not?”
>
> Zhaozhou said, “No.”

* * *

One word, and it has always been counted among the hard cases. Plenty of people have worked on it. Very few seem to have walked straight through.

The sutras say that all sentient beings have Buddha nature, so a dog should have it too. Zhaozhou says no. The monk is stuck right there. Follow the scriptures and say yes, but Zhaozhou has just said no. Follow Zhaozhou and say no, and it does not match the teaching. Neither side is reachable, and the mind cannot find a place to put the question down.

It does not look as though Zhaozhou meant for anyone to slide out along either side. Being stuck here is probably what he intended.

* * *

The “no” does not seem to be a claim that dogs lack Buddha nature, and it does not seem to be asking anyone to decide who is right. It is more like handing you one word to put your whole mind on, again and again, until the mind that has to sort everything into *has* and *has not* wears itself out and lets go.

After a while, a person may begin to see that the answer is probably not on the “yes” side or the “no” side. When the frame that insists on two options loosens, the wall that was blocking the way comes down on its own.

* * *

The questions that keep us stuck in ordinary life are often not that difficult. The way we ask them has already boxed us in, leaving only yes and no as exits. Change the question, or set it down for a while without answering, and a little room appears.
