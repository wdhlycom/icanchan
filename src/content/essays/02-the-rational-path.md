Buddhism has one trait that separates it from most religions: it does not begin by asking you to believe anything.

That sentence needs care. "You don't have to believe" is itself a claim, and agreeing with it is a small act of faith. So it is worth taking apart rather than swapping one slogan for another.

Look at any religion and three pieces usually show up: an account of how the world is, a set of rules for how a person should behave, and a promise about what follows if the rules are kept. Buddhism is not assembled that way. There is no god in the hall waiting to be worshipped. Shakyamuni was a person with a place in the historical record. Rebirth and nirvana are not entrance requirements; nobody asks you to nod before you sit down. In the earliest Buddhism there was not even a creed to memorize. What was offered instead was a practice — sit, follow the breath, watch thoughts come and go — plus a working vocabulary for what the mind does when nobody is pushing it.

The comparison I keep returning to is swimming lessons. A good teacher does not begin with the molecular structure of water. The teacher puts you in the pool.

## Sitting, not praying

Zazen, the practice behind the Japanese word, is not prayer. It is closer to sitting and looking. You sit; you breathe; a thought rises and comes apart. You don't chase it and you don't press it down. You notice: oh, thinking.

Psychology has a name for the move this trains — decentering. The mind learns to step back half a step from the story it is telling. That matters because when people are miserable, the event is often the smaller part of the misery. The larger part is the commentary the mind wraps around it. Something ordinary happens; a sentence arrives about being attacked; now the ordinary thing is aimed at you. Sitting daily, watching daily, wears that shell thinner.

An old Chan saying runs: before awakening, chop wood, carry water; after awakening, chop wood, carry water.

The line is exact. Awakening, looked at closely, is less an acquisition than a recognition. The days without the running commentary were already sufficient. The wood is the same wood, the water the same water. What changed is the noisy circle standing around it.

## What the research actually says

In 1979 an American named Jon Kabat-Zinn opened an outpatient clinic at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center. He was not there to chant over patients. He taught them to meditate, and because hospitals at the time had little to say about whether meditation could treat illness, the course was run along clinical lines. By the 1980s, controlled studies appeared, and the scales for chronic pain, anxiety, and depression did move.

More studies followed, and the claims narrowed. Decentering, for example: can a person put some distance between themselves and the sentence "I am anxious" — not become someone who isn't anxious, but find the gap between the anxiety and the anxious me. That capacity can be measured in experiments, and it can be trained.

Some claims have not held up as well. The idea that mindfulness restructures the brain comes mostly from early studies with small samples and no control group, and later attempts to replicate them have not supported it much. The immunity claim is in worse shape: results point in different directions, and nothing definite can be said. I would rather state that plainly than sell the practice on the strength of it.

My own rule: measure what can be measured, and don't turn the rest into a promise.

Chan is not science. Chan is a method much older than science and much clumsier. The relationship is not that research has now validated meditation. Each can be of use to the other, provided neither dresses up as the other.

---

Which leaves belief.

My answer is to postpone it in both directions. Don't believe it yet. Don't rush to disbelieve it either. Sit for ten minutes and watch your thoughts come and go. If nothing at all happens in those ten minutes, that is still information: you now know whether the seat is empty.

The practice has one property I find useful. It does not stop working depending on whether you believe in it.
