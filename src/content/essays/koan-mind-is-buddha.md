> A monk asked, “Why do you keep saying that this mind is Buddha?”
>
> Mazu said, “To stop a child crying.”
>
> The monk asked, “And when the crying stops?”
>
> Mazu said, “Not mind, not Buddha.”

* * *

This exchange is mostly about how a teacher hands out words.

Mazu does not begin by explaining what a Buddha is. He begins with a child who is crying.

A child wakes at night and cries. Sometimes he is afraid of the dark, sometimes of a shadow, sometimes he just cannot find his mother. An adult says, “Don't be afraid, I'm here,” and the crying gradually stops. “This mind is Buddha” is roughly that sentence. Someone feels that the Buddha is far away, out of reach, and the line settles him down: you do not have to look outside.

But if the child stopped crying long ago and is still clutching the sentence, it becomes a new problem. It was meant to calm him. Over time it turns into a mantra, a standard answer carried around and produced in front of anyone, and he will not put it down.

Mazu's second half is for that. Once the child has stopped crying, he says: not mind, not Buddha.

* * *

These comforting lines are more like medicine than daily food. You take the one that fits the complaint. When the complaint is gone, it should probably be put down. Nobody lives with the medicine jar in his arms.

“This mind is Buddha” answers the urge to look outside. “Not mind, not Buddha” answers the hand that will not let go of an answer. The two sentences are spoken at different times and do different work. Memorize either one and produce it for everybody, and you are back on the old road.

What matters is probably seeing where you are stuck right now and which sentence you need to hear. How well you have memorized either one is secondary.

A sentence that once put your mind at rest is easy to keep. Hear it enough, say it smoothly enough, and it starts to fence you in. You check whether a move fits the sentence before you make it.
