"A day without work, a day without eating."

That was the rule Baizhang Huaihai set for his monastery. No work that day, no meal that day. He meant it literally, and he is said to have kept to it himself after the other monks hid his tools to make him rest.

![A Day Without Work](/illustrations/ill-122.jpg)
In India, monks lived on alms and didn't farm. Moved to China, the arrangement ran into trouble. This was a society that measured a person by the harvest. A monk who didn't work the land looked, to the neighbors, like a grown man eating for free.

It got worse as the monasteries grew wealthy and numerous. The court looked at them with a colder and colder eye, and when the persecutions came, the charge in the official documents kept coming back to the same two words: sitting and eating.

Baizhang put a hoe in the monks' hands. From then on, Chan monasteries grew their own food and cooked it. This wasn't an improvised fix. It turned one thing all the way around: labor was no longer outside practice. The work itself was placed inside it.

* * *

These days the sentence gets pulled out on its own and taped to office walls as motivation. Its original sense took a longer route than *be industrious*.

Baizhang wasn't rushing anyone toward more hours. He was saying practice has no business being set against work. When we picture practice, we picture sitting, chanting, silence. Work is movement, chores, the mundane — and it seems to steal time from the real thing. Baizhang erased the line in the middle. Hauling rocks and sitting in meditation start to look like two faces of one activity.

* * *

When the Huichang persecution came, the court tore down monasteries and ordered monks back to lay life. Traditions that lived on donations fell apart almost overnight. The farming Chan line had land, work, and food, and it lasted.

There isn't much mystery in that. It survived because there was always soil under its feet and a meal at the end of the day — unlike the communities that hung on a supply line, which were left dangling the moment it was cut.

* * *

You probably don't farm. But the *work* is still there: cooking, laundry, the commute, the proposal due Friday. The day is full of it. We tend to treat these as the price of having a life, mind somewhere else, done carelessly so we can get to the part that matters.

Seen another way, they aren't only the preparation before practice. Do them with some care, with the attention actually on them, and you are already inside the thing.

---

**A question to sit with:** Which task today will you rush through as something you have to do — and what would be different if it were practice too?
