Standing in the hallway at eleven at night, you see them: three parcels by the door, one still taped shut since Tuesday.

Ordering is the good part. There is a small lift in the chest when the confirmation screen appears. Then three or four days of a low hum in the background — something is on its way. You open the box and for an hour you are full. Nothing is missing. By the weekend the fullness has drained out, and the thing itself is just a thing on a shelf.

So you browse again. Add to cart, remove it, add it back. Order. Wait. Open. Drain.

The box from Tuesday is still by the door, and there is already a cart open on your phone. The closet is packed. The drawers won't close. And there is still a patch inside that feels unoccupied.

![The Parcel Isn't Even Open](/illustrations/ill-073.jpg)
You have noticed this before. Everyone has. Then a sale comes around and you do it again.

The satisfaction is not fake, which is the trap. It is real. It is just short. It doesn't stay.

Chan pushes the question in one step further: is the thing you want actually the object?

Usually not, or not only. Sometimes what you are buying is the feeling of becoming someone slightly better — the person who owns this, reads this, uses this. Sometimes it is the feeling that the calendar has something in it. Underneath both, maybe a brief impression of not lacking anything. The item does stand-in work for all of that. It holds the feeling's place for a couple of days.

A stand-in has one flaw. It cannot fill the hole it is standing in front of. The empty patch does not respond to more furniture, better furniture, or the right lamp. The more you add, the more clearly you have pointed at it — to yourself. Someone who isn't empty in that spot generally doesn't need to keep topping it up.

* * *

None of this means stop buying things. Objects have uses. Buying something that actually helps you get through a week isn't a moral failure; it's Tuesday.

What is worth watching is the force of the urge — the specific quality of I have to have this now. When it comes up, some people have learned to wait a beat and ask what the empty patch is made of tonight. Is it a long evening with nothing in it? Is it exhaustion? Is it the flat sense that not much is coming? Or is it simply that you want to give yourself something after a hard month?

* * *

Seen clearly, the emptiness loses some of its authority. It stops being a reason and becomes a weather report.

Others keep a simpler rule. Put it in the cart, close the tab, sleep. If it is still there in the morning and you still want it, buy it then. Nothing is lost.

Most things are not still there in the morning. And that one night is usually enough to tell the difference between wanting the thing because it is useful and wanting it because it holds the door shut on a feeling you would rather not stand in.

---

**A question to sit with:** The last thing you couldn't leave in the cart — was it something you needed, or something to look forward to?
