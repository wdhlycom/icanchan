The announcement lands in the team channel at 10:14. A short paragraph, two exclamation marks, then a stream of congratulations, including yours — typed fast, maybe a little too fast.

By 10:30 you're back at your desk and a list is forming. He isn't that strong on the technical side. He's vague in meetings. There was that thing in March that nobody followed up on. You didn't go looking for this list. It assembled itself.

Then, somewhere around item four, the shame arrives. You hear how you sound. You feel small, and then you feel bad about feeling small, which is a whole second shift.

![When a Colleague Gets Promoted](/illustrations/ill-125.jpg)
The shame can wait. It is usually the least useful part of the experience.

Jealousy isn't a character defect. Put a measuring stick in a room and people will start measuring. If the office is arranged around rankings — levels, titles, who got the good project — then jealousy is less a personal failing than a predictable output.

What is more corrosive is the second layer: the belief that you shouldn't be feeling it. Now you are jealous, ashamed of being jealous, and slightly disgusted by the list you just made. Three things at once, none of them going anywhere.

* * *

Chan tends to look at this less at the colleague and more at a small sentence inside you: I'm not as good as him.

Because what happened divides into two parts. Part one: he was promoted. That's a fact. It's in the channel. It's on the org chart. Part two: therefore I am less. That's a story the mind adds without asking, in about a second and a half.

The fact doesn't cut very deep. A company moved a person from one box to another. What cuts is the sentence in the middle, and you wrote that sentence, in a hurry, on very little evidence.

* * *

So the first move isn't to argue with the jealousy. It's to separate the two pieces.

He got promoted. Full stop.

I am now less than him. Separate line.

There is a long stretch of road between those two statements, and most of us cover it in the time it takes to type congratulations.

Then a second question, which takes longer: what am I actually angry about? Is it that he has the job, or is it that the thing I've been working toward for three years still hasn't arrived? Different problems, different remedies. If it's the second — and it usually is, at least partly — then all the energy going into that list is aimed at the wrong thing. Watching him closely will not get you promoted.

* * *

Later, when it comes up again, some people stop doing two things. They stop scolding themselves, and they stop itemizing the other person. They just note it. Ah — measuring again.

That small recognition doesn't dissolve the feeling. It takes a hand off the throttle. The feeling is there, you have seen it, and it is no longer running the rest of the morning.

---

**A question to sit with:** In that jealousy, how much is why him, and how much is why not me yet?
