Everyone has two sentences ready about impermanence. It is the kind of word that sits comfortably in conversation, the way weather does.

Then it lands on you. The job ends in a meeting you didn't see coming. A scan comes back with something on it. A relationship that looked solid is over by the weekend. None of it sent notice.

You knew things change. You still ask the same question: why this, why now, why me. The question isn't really asking for information. It is the sound a person makes when the ground shifts.

![The Word Impermanence](/illustrations/ill-065.jpg)
The hard part isn't understanding. Your head can hold the fact that everything shifts, and your heart can still refuse to accept that the things in your hands might not stay. What sits between the two is mostly the unwillingness to let go.

That is not stupidity. It is the shape of having loved something. It is not the same as clinging, though it comes close enough that most of us mistake one for the other.

* * *

People keep a small private reservation. Things change elsewhere, but probably not here. The job will hold steady. The body will stay as it is. The people around me will still be around. Impermanence sounds like something said to other people, not something that applies at this address.

When it arrives, you don't feel like you are hearing it for the first time. You feel like you have run into it for the first time, and only then do you understand how little you had believed it.

What is strangest is how ordinary the day stays. The bus still comes. Someone at the office asks what you want for lunch. You answer, and it is a perfectly normal answer, and all the while the ground has shifted a little under the sentence.

* * *

The Buddha didn't teach impermanence to make people gloomy. Seeing it early means not being caught completely unprepared when it comes. That seems closer to the point.

And people who have seen it clearly don't seem to cherish less. Knowing that the hot meal, this particular meeting, this ordinary day without pain will not last tends to give them more weight, not less. Someone who has watched a parent forget a name does not need the word explained to them; they simply hold the next conversation a little differently.

* * *

This isn't something you get past by deciding to look on the bright side. It grinds in slowly, over years, and it is not a mood you arrive at once and then keep. While you have something, don't treat it as owed to you. When you lose it, don't treat it as the sky falling. Precisely because it can be lost, this hour is worth living properly.

---

**A question to sit with:** Of the things you are holding tightly right now, is there one you have quietly assumed you will never lose?
