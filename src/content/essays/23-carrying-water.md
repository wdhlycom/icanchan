"Miraculous powers and wondrous function: carrying water and hauling firewood."

That's Layman Pang. He gave away the family fortune and lived with his household making bamboo baskets to get by. Coming from anyone else the line would sound like modesty. From him it lands as a plain report of what he saw.

Picture the yard. Bundles of bamboo drying against a wall, a kettle going, his daughter Lingzhao splitting strips with a knife. Someone visits from a nearby temple, hoping for a word about the higher stages, and gets shown the water jar and the woodpile. The visitor keeps waiting for the metaphor to end. It doesn't.

![Carrying Water, Hauling Wood](/illustrations/ill-101.jpg)
Both words had weight at the time. *Powers* meant the supernatural — the abilities the old texts list. *Wondrous function* meant the effortless working of an awakened mind. People assumed that anyone who practiced far enough would show something remarkable: light, foreknowledge, lotuses appearing out of empty air.

Pang says: stop looking. The top of practice, described honestly, is water and firewood.

* * *

Heard today, it sounds like a punchline that takes the air out of the room. You spend half a lifetime chasing the extraordinary, and someone tells you the extraordinary was mostly something you invented, and that the ordinary is where you were standing the whole time.

There is a particular disappointment in that, if you have been at it a while. You wanted the ceiling to lift. Instead the floor turns out to be solid, which is less exciting and more useful.

It isn't permission to lie down, though. What matters is the whole set of movements packed into those four words. The hands are carrying water and the mind is carrying water. Nothing is split off and sent elsewhere. Most of us do daily work with the body in the room and the mind three rooms ahead — the body hauling the wood, the attention already at dinner. Pang is describing hauling that bundle with body and mind in the same place.

* * *

Powers and wondrous function don't seem to be produced somewhere else and then displayed. When a person does the thing in front of them all the way through, that quality shows up on its own. There is no *I* in it angling for a result, and no task bearing down on the person. Only the thing, moving.

* * *

So someone picks the least impressive item on the list. Put the water on. Sweep the floor. No hurry toward the next thing, no rehearsing the next thing. Just this one. Halfway through, the attention leaves; you notice, and you walk it back.

That, more or less, is what carrying water and hauling wood looks like now.

---

**A question to sit with:** The extraordinary things you have chased all your life — how many were a real need, and how many were just a refusal to admit that ordinary is already enough?
