> Whenever anyone asked Juzhi a question about the Dharma, he said nothing. He simply raised one finger.
>
> Near the end of his life he told his students, “I got one-finger Chan from Tianlong. I have not used it up in a lifetime.”

* * *

There is a second story. A boy at the monastery had watched for so long that he learned the gesture. When visitors asked what the master taught, he raised a finger too. Juzhi heard about it, hid a knife in his sleeve, and called the boy over. “I hear you have learned the Dharma,” he said. The boy started to raise his finger, and Juzhi cut it off. The boy ran out in pain. Juzhi called his name. The boy turned his head, saw Juzhi holding up one finger, and was awakened.

Put the two stories side by side, and the finger is probably not a code. There is nothing hidden inside it waiting for someone to say the matching word.

What the boy learned was a shape: lift the hand, extend one finger. Anyone can copy that. When Juzhi raised his finger, it was probably because there was nothing left to say. Only this gesture remained, and whether he could reproduce it had nothing to do with it.

The shape looks identical. Where it comes from is far apart. One is traced from another person. The other only appears when you have been pushed to the point where no sentence is left.

* * *

When people learn a skill, the first thing they pick up is usually the appearance: how to phrase it, what tone to take when something happens, how to sit. Appearances are easy to copy and easy to use. After enough use, you may no longer be able to tell whether you understand, or have simply memorized the movements.

Juzhi's knife looks as if it cuts the finger. It seems more like it removes the thing the boy was leaning on when he said “I have learned it.” When you claim to know something, there is always something underneath holding you up: a sentence you remember, or a gesture you can perform. In pain, a person has no attention left for appearances. And when he turns back, there is no time to prepare an answer.

The finger is still raised. This time it does not look as though it came from someone else.

* * *

“I have not used it up in a lifetime” does not sound like praise for a clever trick. There is nothing behind that finger to copy. A person can only raise it after arriving somewhere with nothing left to say, and the next time will be another place with nothing left to say. Seen this way, it cannot wear out and cannot settle into a fixed method. A whole life of it, and there is still some left.

---

**Of the movements your hands know very well, which one is a shape you learned, and which one only appeared because you had arrived?**
