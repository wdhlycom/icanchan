What is Buddha?

A dried stick of shit.

What is the meaning of the patriarch's coming from the West?

The cypress tree in the courtyard.

![Why Koans Answer Something Else](/illustrations/ill-099.jpg)
Reading that, most people are lost. The obvious complaint is that it isn't an answer at all. Did the old teachers enjoy being obscure?

A koan isn't a riddle. A riddle has a correct answer, and once you have it you put it down and go to lunch. A koan doesn't hand over an answer. It seems built to walk the mind that must have an explanation to the one place it cannot pass, and leave it there, stopped. Being left there is uncomfortable, and the discomfort appears to be part of the method rather than a flaw in it.

* * *

We spend most of our lives fitting concepts over things. Ask what Buddha is, and the mind expects a definition it can memorize: Buddha is an awakened person, Buddha is something or other. Zen seems to want you to notice for yourself that the concept won't go on.

The harder you try to understand, the clearer it is that you are still using the old method. Then the teacher throws a dried stick of shit across the table and kicks the conceptual road out from under you.

* * *

The kick sounds crude. It isn't meant to humiliate anyone. It is meant to make a turn unavoidable.

When the think-it-through road is blocked, there is a moment where the mind simply stops turning. You know the feeling from ordinary life: the sentence that trails off, the question you suddenly have no reply to, the half second where nothing in the cupboard of ready answers fits. In that stop, people sometimes find themselves nearer the thing than they were while chasing it — because this isn't a matter you solve in a chair. It gets lived out, bit by bit, in the middle of an ordinary day.

* * *

So what a koan asks for is less an answer than attention held over time. Not deduction. You keep the phrase in your chest and carry it, through weeks, through a year. Carried long enough, it stops being a question and becomes part of how you move through the world.

The cypress tree in the courtyard doesn't look like an answer either. It looks more like a hand on your shoulder, turning your face to what is already there. There. Right there.

* * *

Later, opening a koan collection again, some people stop rushing to understand. They keep the phrase in the mouth like a candy that won't dissolve. Whether they understand it matters less than it used to. What stays is the jolt that stopped them, and the small willingness to be stopped again.

---

**A question to sit with:** How much of what matters in a life can't be reasoned out from a chair, and has to be lived out slowly, over years?
