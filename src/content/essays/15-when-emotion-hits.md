Sometimes it is one sentence, one look, and the anger flares.

By the time you are back, the words are already out and the slammed door is still shaking the room. You are sorry. Next time, mostly the same.

What sticks is not only temperament. In that short stretch, you did not get a look at what happened.

![When the Anger Flares](/illustrations/ill-113.jpg)
Emotion rarely reaches the top in one jump. It usually comes in two steps with a gap between them.

The first is the stimulus. The sentence lands and something in the chest is pricked.

The second is the reaction. The surge carries you and the words come out.

Most people press the two steps into one. Stimulus, reaction, no gap at all. Chan works on the gap, on whether it can be found.

* * *

Daniel Goleman called the collapse an amygdala hijack: the emotional brain takes over before the reasoning brain is in the room.

Chan puts it more simply. You and the emotion are not the same thing. There is something beside it, watching.

In ordinary life you are far from that watching. Anger rises and fills the whole room of you, and who is watching and who is watched is hard to tell apart.

So the work has to move earlier, to ordinary days, before there is any fire. Practice noticing the thought when it is small: the slight tightening when a name appears in the inbox, the story you start telling yourself about what that email really meant. That is the same gap, only quiet. By the time a real fire starts, the opening is easier to spot, even for a second. The gap does not open because you want it to. It opens because you have looked at the small versions so often that the large one is recognizable.

A second is enough to pull the hand back from the door.

* * *

When the anger just hits, do not speak yet. Do one thing: put a hand on your chest and feel three heartbeats.

It has no other use. It buys seconds. In those seconds, *I am the anger* becomes *I see the anger*, and that shift may be enough for a different reply. Not a kinder reply, necessarily. Just one that came from you rather than from the surge.

* * *

Emotion is not the opponent. It is a messenger, telling you where you were touched. Under the anger there is usually something smaller and more specific: I was not seen, or that was unfair, or this keeps happening. You do not have to put the messenger out quickly. Leave a gap between it and what you do next.

---

**A question to sit with:** Next time the anger rises, can you hold off for a moment and ask whether you are answering the other person or being led by your own fire?
