> A Vinaya master asked Dazhu Huihai, “Do you still apply effort in your practice?”
>
> “I do.”
>
> “How do you apply it?”
>
> “When hungry, I eat. When tired, I sleep.”
>
> “Everyone does that. Does that mean they apply effort the same way you do?”
>
> “Not the same.”
>
> “Why not?”
>
> “When they eat, they will not let themselves eat. A hundred demands. When they sleep, they will not let themselves sleep. A thousand calculations. That is why it is not the same.”

* * *

There is almost no machinery in this exchange. The answer is so ordinary that it sounds like no answer at all.

But those two phrases — a hundred demands, a thousand calculations — hold an ordinary day up to the light very clearly. The food is in your mouth and your mind has already drifted elsewhere. You are lying in bed and your head is still running through the day. Your hands are doing the thing and you are not entirely there with them.

Seen this way, applying effort may not mean adding one more activity. It may mean gathering the person and the attention back into the one thing already underway.

* * *

When people hear “practice,” an image usually arrives first: a block of time set aside, a particular way of sitting, some movement you do not normally make. What Dazhu describes happens several times a day to everybody. It is too ordinary even to remember.

That is exactly where the difficulty is. It is so familiar that we do not take it seriously, and the less seriously we take it, the more easily the mind slips away while we are doing it.

Hunger and tiredness are messages the body sends on its own. They do not go around in circles and they do not need to be taught. Follow them, eat and sleep, and for that stretch the person seems to be all in one piece.

And the times we are least willing to follow them are often the most trivial times of all. We decide it is not worth stopping for.

**Is there one small thing you do with your whole self, without splitting half of it off somewhere else?**
